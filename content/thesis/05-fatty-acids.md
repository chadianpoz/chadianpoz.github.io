<h2>5.1 Abstract</h2>

<p>The acid-brine lakes of Western Australia are a series of chemically diverse, hypersaline ponds which undergo wet-dry cycling, making them a mineralogical analog to Mars <em>&gt;</em>3 Ga BP, where such cycling likely occurred. In a targeted search for biosignature patterns in fatty acids we approach salt crusts and aluminum clays from three WA lake sites as endmember analogs for wet ancient Mars and include samples from the arid Yungay region of the Atacama Desert as analog endmembers for an arid recent Mars. To detect fatty acids, we use standalone pyrolysis gas chromatography mass spectrometry (pyro-GC-MS) and thermochemolysis GC-MS with tetramethyl ammonium hydroxide (TMAH) as a reagent to liberate organics from the mineral matrix and to hydrolyze and methylate macromolecular carbon. Our results show that the matrix type affects the recovery of fatty acids by TMAH. It is highly effective in releasing even-over-odd patterned concentrations of C<sub>10</sub>-C<sub>18</sub> fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) in gypsum crusts from both WA and the Yungay, and in releasing C<sub>8</sub>-C<sub>14</sub> FAMEs from the Yungay iron-aluminosilicates. The WA aluminosilicates and halite crust demonstrate substantially less recovery efficiency with TMAH relative to their total organic carbon (TOC). Our study indicates that the TMAH experiments onboard the Mars <em>Curiosity</em> rover’s SAM instrument and the planned <em>Rosalind</em> <em>Franklin</em> rover’s MOMA instrument may be better suited for biosignature detection in evaporitic calcium sulfate (CaSO<sub>4</sub>) materials from which patterns of high FAME length are well recovered relative to halite and aluminosilicate clays.</p>

<h2>5.2 Introduction</h2>

<p>The search for organic compounds on Mars has been a focus of planetary science and astrobiology since the 1970s, when the twin Viking spacecraft landed on the planet [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-31'>31</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-235'>235</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-236'>236</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-237'>237</a>]. At the time, the pyrolysis-GC-MS instruments onboard were not deemed to have detected any indigenous organic compounds, but interpreting detection of organics on Mars proved more complex than initially thought [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-238'>238</a>] in part due to the chemical matrix present, i.e., the reactivity of the perchlorate-rich regolith [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-239'>239</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-240'>240</a>]. Conclusive detection of indigenous Martian organics was not announced until 2013, a year after the Curiosity rover analyses of the Cumberland sample in the Sheepbed mudstone [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-241'>241</a>].</p>

<p>Fatty acids or, more broadly, carboxylic acids and lipids, are of particular interest due to their ubiquity in life on Earth and their ability to display patterns of biogenicity in their distribution [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-242'>242</a>]. An ongoing challenge in the search to identify biosignatures is to identify patterns in organic molecules that point to biological rather than abiotic origins. For instance, one hypothesized biosignature in fatty acids is the dominance of an abundance pattern that deviates from abiotic synthetic routes; terrestrial life produces fatty acids dominated by even-number carbon chain lengths rather than odd-number of chain lengths [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-243'>243</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-244'>244</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-245'>245</a>]. Therefore, detecting a similar predominance of even-numbered fatty acids on Mars would suggest that abiotic synthesis is not the dominant mechanism governing the organic pool and may indicate biological synthesis. This detected inventory of biologically synthesized organics would be distinct in distribution (higher concentrations of smaller organics with little functional or even-odd preference) from those potentially generated on Mars by Fisher-Troph type abiotic reactions [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-246'>246</a>] or those delivered in meteoritic macromolecular organic matter [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-247'>247</a>]. While no single analytical approach can prove the existence of extraterrestrial biology [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-248'>248</a>], identifying inconsistencies in fatty acid distributions with known abiotic formation pathways may end up being a central argument in that debate [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-249'>249</a>].</p>

<p>In this study, we analyzed samples from three acid saline lakes in Western Australia and two samples from the Atacama Desert in Chile, as both locations are considered Mars analog environments [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-218'>218</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-250'>250</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-251'>251</a>]. Our goal is to understand the analytical, mineralogical, and taphonomic (preservation) influences on biosignature pattern detection in fatty acids, due to their ubiquity in terrestrial life. To do this, we tested two analytical approaches: tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH) thermochemolysis gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis and pyrolysis-GC-MS without derivatization to assess the FA preservation and detectability in these samples. By examining these analog environments and analytical approaches, we aim to provide insights relevant to current and future Mars missions, including<em> Curiosity</em> and<em> Rosalind Franklin</em>.</p>

<p>The acid saline lakes of Western Australia were selected as analog sites because their evaporite-rich environments closely resemble Martian lacustrine systems that experienced water loss during the planet’s transition to arid conditions. During the Noachian-Hesperian transition, approximately 3.7 Ga ago, Mars’ surface was at its most liquid water-rich and at its most habitable state [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-252'>252</a>]. As the planet became more arid, some of these waters evaporated and remaining habitable lacustrine systems became areas of high concentration for clays, salts, and, possibly, organic compounds, much like evaporites and salterns on Earth [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-253'>253</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-254'>254</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-255'>255</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-47'>47</a>]. The Mars<em> Curiosity</em> rover landed in Gale Crater in 2012, its ChemCam instrument detected calcium and magnesium sulfates of up to 50 and 36 wt%, respectively, in the bedrock of Gale Crater’s Murray formation, consistent with deposition by ancient, evapoconcentrating lakes of the Hesperian [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-47'>47</a>]. Recent observations indicate the presence of these wet-dry cycling lakes through the Noachian-Hesperian transition and across the smectite to sulfate strata, evidenced by the presence sulfate-enriched junctions of polygonal desiccation cracks [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-48'>48</a>]. Once standing water fully evaporated, salts were left behind as evaporite deposits, like the salt crusts observed in the Western Australia study sites. However, those salts on Mars experienced further physical alteration, turning what may have been crusts into highly altered polygonal cracks with sulfate-enriched junctions.</p>

<p>As the Martian salts underwent aging, weathering, diagenesis [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-256'>256</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-257'>257</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-258'>258</a>], and eventually high UV flux and dehydration they resembled the dry, altered evaporites and clays of the Yungay region in Chile’s Atacama Desert [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-259'>259</a>]. In the Yungay region, life is slow to grow and reproduce due to the low availability of water molecules and nutrients, and the high UV flux [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-260'>260</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-138'>138</a>]. Due to these properties, along with its old, relatively Mars-like mineralogy, the Atacama, and particularly the hyperarid Yungay region, has been deemed and often used as an analog for present-day Mars in multiple studies [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-131'>131</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-261'>261</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-132'>132</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-262'>262</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-133'>133</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-263'>263</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-134'>134</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-135'>135</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-264'>264</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-265'>265</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-30'>30</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-266'>266</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-136'>136</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-137'>137</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-260'>260</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-138'>138</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-259'>259</a>]. Sulfates are known to be highly effective at preserving organic biosignature compounds in certain contexts [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-267'>267</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-164'>164</a>]. Similarly, phyllosilicate clays, in particular smectites, can preserve organics effectively through their binding to the clay’s cationic interlayer [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-235'>235</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-268'>268</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-269'>269</a>]. Halite has been shown to preserve fatty acids in the Atacama after extraction by the Bligh Dyer method [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-259'>259</a>] Both sulfate crystal salts and halite are well known to preserve cells in inclusions [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-270'>270</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-271'>271</a>].</p>

<p>Significant work has been performed on the lakes of Western Australia (WA) as Mars analogs [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-272'>272</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-14'>14</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-273'>273</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-218'>218</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-270'>270</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-271'>271</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-200'>200</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-274'>274</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-275'>275</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-276'>276</a>], establishing the evapoconcentration mechanisms in these acid lakes which often leave evaporites with inclusions of living microbes [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-270'>270</a>]. Microbial metagenomes were obtained from WA acid lakes [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-274'>274</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-276'>276</a>], and GC-MS lipid analyses have been previously performed on sediments from four acidic lakes near Norseman, WA using Bligh Dyer extractions to find alkanes, fatty acids, alcohols, and sterols [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-200'>200</a>]. TMAH analyses of those samples, which had TOCs of similar or higher levels to those in this study were also performed, yielding more FAMEs in the circumneutral than the acid lake samples, attributing some preservation to the clay present [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-275'>275</a>].</p>

<p>The Mars Science Laboratory <em>Curiosity</em> rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012, is equipped with the Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-277'>277</a>]. SAM contains a gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (GC-MS) and tunable laser spectrometer (TLS) as well as a sample processing and gas handling system. The SAM instrument includes two derivatization agents, N-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (MTBSTFA)</p>

<figure class='inline-eq'><img src='/thesis/05-02.webp' alt='Figure' loading='lazy'></figure>

<p>tizing amino acids, while the latter is a thermochemolysis agent aimed at releasing longchain aliphatic hydrocarbons from the mineral matrix and methylating fatty acids to form a fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) which has the same carbon chain length as the reactant fatty acid [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-275'>275</a>]. This payload is accompanied by complementary instruments and modes which first determine a sample’s elemental content (via ChemCam) and mineralogy (via ChemMin and SAM’s evolved gas analysis mode) [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-278'>278</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-279'>279</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-280'>280</a>] to determine if the sample will be submitted to SAM’s more resource intensive analyses. The ExoMars<em> Rosalind</em> <em>Franklin</em> rover, slated to land in Oxia Planum, Mars, in 2029 is also equipped with TMAH, making the capabilities of this thermochemolysis compound relevant for continued study in Mars analog locations [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-281'>281</a>]. In addition, TMAH will be included on the<em> Dragonfly</em> mission to Saturn’s hydrocarbon-rich moon Titan where the mineralogy is largely unknown [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-282'>282</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-283'>283</a>].</p>

<p>The SAM instrument aboard Curiosity has performed the TMAH experiment once, successfully identifying aromatic hydrocarbons likely cleaved by the TMAH reagent from insoluble macromolecular organic material (Williams et al. <em>in</em> <em>review</em>). This material is likely indigenous to the Martian samples, as it is detected in numerous instrument modes, while the provenance of other compounds only detected by the GC-MS, downstream of the contaminant-shedding Tenax<sup>®</sup> hydrocarbon trap, is unresolved [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-45'>45</a>]. One unpunctured cup of TMAH remains on-board SAM, awaiting use upon selection of an appropriate Martian sample.</p>

<h2>5.3 Materials and Methods</h2>

<h3>5.3.1 Sample Collection</h3>

<p>Geologically modern samples were collected from three sites in WA in August 2022. Lake Site 1 located near Salmon Gums and closely associated to the Cowan palaeovalley channel (LS1, 33.05396 S, 121.6753 E), Lake Site 2 located in the Lake Campion Nature Reserve in the Deborah palaeovalley channel (LS2, 31.1449 °S, 118.3368 °E), and Lake Gounter located in the Lake Gounter Nature Reserve in the Camm palaeovalley channel (LG, 32.406183 S, 118.838389 E) (Figure 5.1a-c). This sampling was part of Oceans</p>

Insets show examples of crusts at these locations; analyzed samples were harvested using a solvent cleaned metal scoop, not handled by hand.</figcaption></figure>

<p>Across Space and Time (OAST), a multi-institutional project which sampled over 30 lakes in Western Australia for geochemistry, organic chemistry, metabolic activity, cultures, emitted metabolic gas, transcriptomes and genomes. As part of a separate effort, geologically ancient samples were collected from the Yungay region of the Atacama Desert in Chile in 2016 (AT, 24.0623 °S, 69.8717 °W) (Figure 5.1d). In both cases, surface sampling was performed similarly using clean methods, with an ethanol-wiped spatula used to move sample into a polypropylene tube. Sampling of the two lacustrine salt crust layers in Western Australia required excavation of the crust from beneath the liquid brine using a shovel (to break and obtain the crust), whereby an untouched region of crust was then broken off into the tube. Samples were kept frozen at -80°C until analysis in 2023.</p>

<figure class='inline-eq'><img src='/thesis/05-04.webp' alt='Figure' loading='lazy'></figure>

<p>First, the two intact salt crusts from LS2 and LG were washed with methanol to remove mud, brine, and any organics bound to the sample exterior. Then, all samples were powdered in an ashed mortar and pestle for three minutes, leaving them visibly homogenous, and placed into sealed, ashed glassware refrigerated overnight. The next day, 5 mg of powdered samples were individually weighed and placed into nonreactive metal cups for use with the Frontier Lab 3030D pyrolyzer (Fukushima, Japan). To target fatty acids, immediately before analysis, 5 µL of tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH) in methanol and an internal standard of 0.5 µL of naphthalene at 50 mM in dichloromethane was added was added to each sample via glass syringe. Each sample was flash pyrolyzed to 500°C for 0.5 minutes The gas product was carried from the system directly to the gas chromatograph (GC) via helium flow gas at a 3 mL/min rate with a 20:1 split. A new, conditioned Restek MXT-5 capillary GC column (Bellefonte, Pennsylvania, USA) was used in an Agilent 7890A-5975C inertXL GC-MS (Santa Clara, California, USA) for these TMAH analyses. The MXT-5 column has a 0.25 mm internal diameter and 0.23 mm thick Crossbond 5% diphenyl, 95% dimethylpolysiloxane stationary phase. Targeted alkane analyses were also performed, using a standalone pyro-GC-MS analysis with no TMAH agent, the sample was flash pyrolyzed at 950°C for 0.5 minutes. A mass spectrometer scan mode targeting FAMEs and alkanes fragment masses was used to enhance detection capability in scanning for ions, generating a selected ion chromatogram (SIC) in addition to the total ion chromatogram (TIC).</p>

<p>Peak identification was performed by retention time matching followed by fragmentation comparison to the NIST electron ionization (EI) library. Data analysis was performed by integrating the FAME peak areas in the selected ion chromatogram of m/z 74. These peaks were normalized via the naphthalene internal standard peak area.</p>

<h3>5.3.3 Scanning electron microscopy and elemental analysis (SEM/EA)</h3>

<p>Solid samples were first mounted and sputtered with gold for analysis by SEM/EA. Samples were analyzed using a Phenom ProX G5 tabletop environmental SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Elemental analyses were performed quantitatively with a 3 mm spot size, an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, and a beam current of 2.25-2.33 nA under vacuum.</p>

<h3>5.3.4 Lake dissolved ions and geochemical properties</h3>

<p>Lake waters were analyzed via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP) by ACZ Laboratories (Steamboat Springs, CO, USA). Water activity was obtained via the AQUALAB 4TE chilled dewpoint meter (Addium Inc, Pullman, Washington, USA). Calibration and stabilization procedures were performed on the AQUALAB system to ensure consistency, and cleaning was performed regularly in the field.</p>

<h3>5.3.5 ATP assay</h3>

<p>Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) was analyzed in both the solid and liquid samples within 48 hours of sample collection in a near-field clean setup. Analysis was performed with reagents from Roche Diagnostics (Basel, Switzerland) and tris with EDTA as a buffer for extraction (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Procedures from Roche were modified as described elsewhere [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-162'>162</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-174'>174</a>]. Briefly, all liquid samples were unfiltered, and all solid samples were pulverized manually before extraction in tris buffer. Extraction involved submersion in boiling water for three minutes before cooling for three minutes and one minute of centrifugation.</p>

<h3>5.3.6 Carbon analyses</h3>

<p>Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) in the overlying brine as non-purgeable organic carbon was measured via high-temperature catalytic oxidation using a Shimadzu TOC-LCSN analyzer [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-284'>284</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-285'>285</a>].</p>

<p>Total inorganic carbon (TIC) was measured coulometrically using a UIC (Coulometrics) 5017 CO<sub>2</sub> coulometer coupled with an AutoMate automated carbonate preparation device (AutoMateFX.com). Approximately 15 mg of sample is weighed into septum top tubes and placed into the AutoMate carousel. A double needle assembly is used to purge the sample vial of atmospheric gas using CO<sub>2</sub>-free nitrogen carrier gas. Acid is then injected into the sample vial and evolved CO<sub>2</sub> is carried through a silver nitrate scrubber to the coulometer where total C as CO<sub>2</sub> is measured.</p>

<p>For total carbon (TC), samples are loaded into tin capsules and placed in a 50-position automated Zero Blank sample carousel on a Carlo Erba NA1500 CNHS elemental analyzer. After flash combustion in a quartz column containing chromium oxide and silvered cobaltous/cobaltic oxide at 1020°C in an oxygen-rich atmosphere, the sample gas is transported in a He carrier stream and passes through a hot reduction column (650°C) consisting of reduced elemental copper to remove oxygen. The effluent stream then passes through a chemical (magnesium perchlorate) trap to remove water. The stream then passes through a 0.7 M GC column at 125 °C that separates the N<sub>2</sub> and CO<sub>2</sub> gases. Finally, the gases pass through a thermal conductivity detector that measures the size of the pulses of N<sub>2</sub> and CO<sub>2</sub>.</p>

<p>TOC was determined by subtraction of the TIC from the TC.</p>

<h2>5.4 Results</h2>

<h3>5.4.1 Site Characteristics</h3>

<p>The three lakes from which samples for this study were taken had distinct ionic compositions and variable DOC levels which changed from day to day (Table 5.1). The pH of the lakes varied from mildly to hyperacidic. Two of these lakes selected for this study had salt crusts under their surface, Lake Site 2 and Lake Gounter.</p>

<p>The Yungay region of the Atacama Desert has long been used as a Mars analogue due to its age and extremely low precipitation. Sample AT232 used in this study was collected from such an area on the southwest slope of a hill selected based on the location’s use prior use in other analogue studies [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-30'>30</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-137'>137</a>] with precise location dictated by avoidance of prior anthropogenic contamination, and sampling direction to avoid wind-blown contamination from the sampling team into the site. The sample was collected similarly (both in location and methodology) to sample AT44A1 08 from Skelley et al., (2007), with little visible</p>

<p>gypsum on the top crust of the sample, but clearly visible within the top 1-3 cm collected for this study.</p>

<h3>5.4.3 TMAH Thermochemolysis and Pyrolysis GC-MS</h3>

<p>To search for FAs in the form of alkanes and FAMEs in the solid samples we used pyrolysis- GC-MS and TMAH thermochemolysis GC-MS analysis. In selected ion chromatograms (SIC) for m/z value 74, the fragment mass which corresponds to a singly ionized methylated acid group, a pattern of elution is clear for FAMEs and was supported by retention time matching to a FAME standard (Figure 5.3). The CaSO<sub>4</sub> crust sample from Lake Gounter has an even-over-odd chain length FAME pattern in the SIC while that of halite from Lake Site 2 showed no SIC pattern of FAMEs above blank levels. Standalone pyrolysis-GC-MS data showed no fatty acid (as expected, since hydrocarbons are targeted with this technique) chromatographic peak signal above the analytical blank for any of the samples analyzed (other than the alkane standard used for retention time identification), nor was there spectral evidence of their presence above blank level. MTBSTFA analysis was attempted for detection of amino acids, but the analysis of a standard of amino acids failed to show any bisilated amino acid products and data from samples were not readily analyzable due to the abundance of bisilated side products.</p>

<p>Chromatographic peaks were integrated, and samples were quantified as ng of FAME per mg of sample (Figure 5.4). Figure 5.4 includes the three major categories of sample, the Yungay and WA CaSO<sub>4</sub> samples with high levels of FAME with lengths<em> &gt;</em>C<sub>14</sub> and no C<sub>8</sub> present, the Yungay and WA aluminosilicate clay samples with no FAMEs longer than C<sub>14</sub>, some with relatively high levels of C<sub>8</sub> and C<sub>10</sub>-C<sub>13</sub>, and the WA NaCl with very low FAME abundance and only three FAMEs detectable (C<sub>11</sub>, C<sub>13</sub>, and C<sub>14:1</sub>, the only instance of a saturated fatty acids detected in this dataset). The two CaSO<sub>4</sub>-rich samples, the Yungay and Lake Gounter, and the Yungay grains exhibit higher concentrations of even-numbered FAMEs than odd-numbered FAMEs. The WA aluminosilicate clay samples also display this pattern, but less distinctly, in the complete absence of a C<sub>9</sub> FAME detection and low levels of C<sub>11</sub> FAME.</p>

<h2>5.5 Discussion</h2>

<p>The three WA samples were collected to represent different minerals in a Mars analog environment, as a part of a larger study of more than 30 lakes to determine geological, chemical, and biological properties of the acid saline lakes of Western Australia with regards to their relevance as an analog of ancient Mars or other salt-rich ocean worlds. The Atacama samples were obtained prior to this study and were taken from cold storage to be analyzed as a Mars evaporite analog endmember without modern wet-dry cycling. The LS2 and LG samples were used in this study as modern end members which have NaCl and CaSO<sub>4</sub> salt crusts respectively, representative of sulfates and chlorides on Mars at the time of their formation. These WA samples are likely to be the minerals halite and gypsum based on their composition, presentation in the crust, and distinctive morphology. While all the lakes sampled are have day to day variability in pH and salinity, as well as seasonal variability, LS2 is the most acidic of the three in this study with a pH of 2.97 and a water activity of 0.7545, like that observed at the LS1 (<em>a</em><sub>w</sub> 0.7524, but a higher pH of 5.25) site where the WA clays were obtained (Table 5.1). The ATP concentrations obtained of the overlying brine are of generally similar concentration across days but is certainly attenuated by the presence of salts (which change in concentration across days) which are known to interfere with the assay used for ATP detection.</p>

<p>Comparing the SEM images (Figure 5.2) of the two CaSO<sub>4</sub> samples, Yungay crust and LG Crust demonstrate distinctly different shapes. The former is less geometrically ordered and even rounded with a surface covered in flakes of what appear to be clay, while the latter is a rectangular prism with sharply defined edges. It is likely given the porosity of the Yungay crust that aqueous alteration of this mineral has occurred, whereas the WA’s crystal morphology along with its location in an actively wet-dry cycling hypersaline lake, indicates it is a primary mineral, likely gypsum. The Yungay Crust also contains flakes of clay-like material, likely the same Fe-aluminosilicates analyzed as the bulk of the Yungay Grains. The clay samples from WA had generally similar morphologies, and with increasing dryness the morphology transitions from fractured surfaces to cleaved flakes of clay. The WA Dry Clay had small carbon-rich fibers visible in the SEM/EA analysis presumed to be fungal filaments, indicating that extant life is likely present in these WA clay samples. These platelets are visible in both of the Yungay samples, but primarily characterize the Yungay grains, which are of aluminosilicate composition like the WA clays. While the Yungay grains also include iron, the WA samples include qualitatively more sodium and magnesium, reflective of the elemental composition of the overlying WA brine, and some iron. In summary, the Western Australia and Yungay samples represent modern and ancient salt crusts and clays which we use as analogs of ancient and modern Mars respectively.</p>

<p>For this study, we chose to use a flash pyrolysis technique which heated the sample to the desired temperature almost instantly. The SAM instrument is limited to a ramp rate of 35°C min<sup>-1</sup> due to power limitations. Other SAM optimization studies have shown that a flash pyrolysis rapid ramp rate is more effective at extracting trace organics from rock than a slow ramp rate in both standalone pyrolysis and TMAH analyses for FAMEs (Williams et al., 2019).</p>

<p>The detectability of FAME biosignature patterns in the CaSO<sub>4</sub> samples is uniquely elevated relative to the other samples studied here. While all the samples contain presumably biotic TOC these two samples are the only ones in the study which demonstrate clear evenover-odd carbon number preference up to C<sub>18</sub> and which show a distribution not skewed towards shorter chain lengths, as would be expected in an abiotically-generated sample of fatty acids or in a highly degraded organic signal. The remaining aluminosilicate samples, both the Na- and Mg-rich WA and the Fe-rich Yungay, show a slight even-over-odd preference, but with a bias towards shorter chain length fatty acids until C<sub>8</sub>. The Yungay Fe-aluminosilicates were more effective in availing a higher chain length even-over-odd signal than the WA aluminosilicates.</p>

<p>The presence of an even-over-odd signal in fatty acids in the form of FAMEs indicates either modern, or well-preserved organics from cellular metabolism [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-286'>286</a>]. Long-chain fatty acids <em>&gt;</em>C<sub>18</sub> which would suggest plant sources [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-287'>287</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-200'>200</a>], but these were not detected, suggesting against the inclusion of a plant signal in these lakes, despite the presence of plants near the LS2 site. In the Yungay this even-over-odd signal may also be evidence of</p>

<figure><img src='/thesis/05-18.webp' alt='Figure 5.5: Total fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) concentrations in each sample divided by the total organic carbon (TOC) of each sample is displayed to represent the unitless relative recoverability o' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Figure 5.5: Total fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) concentrations in each sample divided by the total organic carbon (TOC) of each sample is displayed to represent the unitless relative recoverability of FAMEs among samples with differing mineral matrices.</figcaption></figure>

<p>a modern microbial presence, perhaps by slow growing endolithic microbes, however the lack of detectable ATP in these samples after solid extraction and bioluminescence assay immediately after collection may contradict this possibility. In addition to these analyses on this sample set, previous studies in the Yungay have indicated that the area is “radiocarbon dead” for over 40,000 years [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-288'>288</a>] lacking any considerable biological fractionation of carbon, and that the D/L ratio racemization of aspartic acid has occurred in upper soils, pointing to residence times of 10<sup>3</sup>-10<sup>5</sup> years [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-137'>137</a>]. Taken with the extremely dry conditions and low biomass in the upper soils, recent studies have concluded that lipids and their patterns in these samples are indeed well preserved and not derived from recent biological activity [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-259'>259</a>]. Therefore, it is likely that this signal is from a period of older microbial activity when water may have been available for more rapid reproduction and metabolism. In the WA samples it is likely that the organics are modern as microbial activity is presently occurring as evidenced by high ATP concentrations in many of the overlying brines. However, we note it is indeed possible that this high ATP is due to salt-induced preservation and evapoconcentration, or to its accumulation as a stress response, as suggested in previous work by our team [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>]. We hypothesize that the DOM is then evapoconcentrated during dry periods and trapped within the crust matrix as salt “crashes out” of solution and precipitates on the surface of the previous dry season’s crust [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-14'>14</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-289'>289</a>].</p>

<p>The lack of FAME detection above C<sub>18</sub> in chain length in any of the samples in this study indicates the possibility that the pool of organics detected using these methods can be entirely attributed to microbial life. However, non-microbial life is prevalent around these lakes. Previous studies have shown fatty acids attributed to cutaneous waxes, i.e., nearby plant life, are detectable in clays in WA when extracted using conventional organic geochemical lipid extraction and detection methods [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-200'>200</a>]. We do not observe any lipids explicitly indicative of these non-microbial processes. The presence of predominantly saturated FAMEs, with the only unsaturated FA detected in our study being a single low concentration detection of C<sub>14:1</sub> FAME in the LS2 NaCl indicates that breakdown may have begun within the fatty acid pool detected, where biologically critical unsaturated fatty acids break down to saturated fatty acids.</p>

<p>Next, we compare the efficacy of the TMAH method in extracting organic carbon from samples of differing mineral matrices. Figure 5.5 shows total FAME concentration normalized (divided) by total organic carbon in each sample. If fatty acids constitute a roughly equal amount in each TOC pool, then the quotient would provide a measure of the efficacy of TMAH thermochemolysis reaction and subsequent GC-MS analysis at releasing and detecting biological organic carbon trapped in the mineral matrix (in units of ng/mg of FAME per wt% TOC). The Fe-aluminosilicates of the Yungay grains seem to have the highest quotient of FAME concentration to TOC, indicating an enhanced capability for the TMAH thermochemolysis method to extract and quantify fatty acids from this mineral. In the Yungay crust and WA calcium sulfate crust we see near equal efficacy using TMAH, despite the disparate ages and possible secondary mineralization of the Yungay crust.</p>

<p>Some of the efficacy of the TMAH in the Yungay CaSO<sub>4</sub> crust may be attributed to the Fe-aluminosilicate material on the exterior of the crust visible in the SEM (Figure 5.2e). The modern, more recently hydrated aluminosilicates from WA, with Mg and Na present, have less efficacy in FAME detectability with TMAH than the gypsum crust. Within the WA clays, a trend emerges where the dryest WA Sandy Clay has worse FAME recoverability relative to its TOC, with the WA Dry Clay next, and the WA Flow Clay with the best FAME recoverability relative to its TOC. This pattern has the more recently brine covered clay, i.e., that which is closest to the waterline of LS1, with a higher quotient of FAME recoverability. This may indicate that the drying process causes the fatty acids to be less recoverable from the matrix, however this trend is contradicted by the Fe-aluminosilicate from the extremely dry Yungay which has the highest recoverability of all the samples. Notably, the morphology of the highest-recovery WA aluminosilicate clay and the highestrecovery sample overall, the Yungay Fe-aluminosilicate, have similar plate morphologies visible in the SEM imagery (Figure 5.2). If this Yungay Fe-aluminosilicate is interpreted to be clay, the clay’s charged surface properties are an explanation for the elevated FAME recovery in the sample. The WA NaCl crust from Lake Site 2 showed the greatest incompatibility with FAME recovery using this TMAH method. We suggest that the 500°C experiment temperature is not high enough to decompose the NaCl mineral, as its melting point is at 800°C, that there are no water molecules in the dehydrated NaCl for TMAH to solvate and hydrolyze, and that therefore very little organic matter is liberated from the crust.</p>

<p>The failure of the standalone pyro-GC-MS analysis to yield alkanes in the samples was surprising, as previous studies have been successful with similar TOC weight percentages (Table 1 of Williams et al. 2019 [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-275'>275</a>]), but potentially showing the limitations of reagentless, unextracted reactions on complex, salt- and clay-rich mineral matrices. While the preservation potential of organics within these minerals may be high, at least for sulfates and silicates, the recovery of organics from those minerals may be equally challenging in the sense that “unlocking” the organics from the preserving mineral matrix is more difficult. Minerals that seem to well-preserve organics may “lock them away” from low temperature reactions and thus from nondestructive thermal analysis. This necessitates the use of agents like TMAH, which lower the thermal destructive potential by utilizing thermochemolytic properties to break bonds without destroying the fatty acids. Simple pyrolysis at 1000°C may also cause reaction of organics and the salt-rich mineral matrix which could destroy fatty acid signals or may simply not liberate them at all. While previous studies may have shown the efficacy of standalone pyro-GC-MS analyses in some low-biomass Mars analog samples, we assert that a salt-rich mineral matrix may complicate that finding, and that TMAH thermochemolysis performs substantially better for the recovery of indigenous organics.</p>

<h2>5.6 Conclusions</h2>

<p>The presence of sulfate veins and nodules on in Gale Crater is attributed to wet-dry cycling lakes [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-46'>46</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-48'>48</a>]. The results of our study of Earth-analog wet-dry lake systems indicate these minerals are an optimal target for detecting the presence of fatty acid biosignature patterns as FAMEs using TMAH. This may be performed <em>in</em> <em>situ</em> in Gale crater with the <em>Curiosity</em> rover, or with the upcoming <em>Rosalind</em> <em>Franklin</em> rover. Furthermore, though the presence of any primary CaSO<sub>4</sub> mineral or crystalline form may be promising for finding intact microbial inclusions as well as defining the providence of liquid water, our study shows that even aqueously altered, potentially secondary minerals, like the Yungay crust still preserve these organics and their molecular biosignature patterns. Future analyses by these rovers must be supported by laboratory analyses of analog materials like this study in order to understand both the geochemical and biological systems which cause the observed phenomenon, but also to disambiguate analytical interferences, variations, and to build inter-method libraries to support <em>in</em> <em>situ</em> analyses by mass spectrometers with numerous modes. Sulfate-rich samples ideal for detectability of biosignature patterns as described in this work have been accessible to the <em>Curiosity</em> rover [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-46'>46</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-290'>290</a>], as well as iron-rich smectites [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-279'>279</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-44'>44</a>]. But despite the capabilities of <em>in</em> <em>situ</em> analysis tools, sample return from Mars by NASA and ESA, or by CNSA may still be first to obtain a clear picture of the trace organic profile and its context in the surrounding mineralogy to determine if any biosignature patterns in FAMEs constitute convincing detection of ancient or extant life.</p>
