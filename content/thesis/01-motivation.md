<h2>1.1 The Search for Extraterrestrial Life</h2>

<p>Many humans wonder whether life, the most dominant chemical process on the surface of Earth, has taken hold elsewhere in the universe, outside the bounds of our planet. It seems possible to many astrobiologists (those of a deeply interdisciplinary field composed of geologists, chemists, biologists, planetary scientists, astronomers, and more) that the phenomenon we call “life” has taken hold on other planetary bodies in our solar system.</p>

<p>In order to narrow down their search for life, astrobiologists look for what planetary bodies have in common with Earth, the most important being the presence of liquid water [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-1'>1</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-2'>2</a>]. In addition to water, the presumed presence of organic matter delivered by meteorites on the surfaces of these bodies provides a similar set of building blocks from which life may have originated [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-3'>3</a>]. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that if life exists or existed on other planetary bodies in our solar system, particularly on the water-rich, or once water-rich worlds of Europa, Enceladus, and Mars, it is or was water-based and organic [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-4'>4</a>], with life on Mars potentially sharing heritage with life on Earth.</p>

<p>Therefore, in this work, we focus on organic compound detection, and in particular, the detection of amino acids and fatty acids. Amino acids and fatty acids are found in every living cell on Earth as the building blocks of proteins and cell lipid bilayer respectively. Amino acids exist across our solar system in carbonaceous meteoritic material, cometary material, and some asteroids, and are therefore very likely to be present on the surfaces of other rocky bodies [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-5'>5</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-6'>6</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-7'>7</a>]. In fact, astrochemists believe isomers of glycine to be present in distant gas clouds elsewhere in our galaxy [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-8'>8</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-9'>9</a>]. This suggests that the same building blocks are present for icy and rocky planetary bodies around the many stars in our observable universe. Thus, the techniques we present here and the compounds which we target may indeed be useful not only for the exploration of our own solar system, but also for exploration of solar systems across the universe.</p>

<h2>1.2 Salts in our Solar System</h2>

<p>Inorganic salts abound on the rocky bodies of our solar system [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-10'>10</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-11'>11</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-12'>12</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-13'>13</a>]. In the context of the search for life they are linked to habitability, modulating the availability of water for cellular use since they can become hydrated or dissolve into solution easily [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-14'>14</a>]. On Earth, we see this can change the osmotic pressure outside a cell, putting pressure on it to adapt its biochemistry to extreme conditions [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-15'>15</a>]. Indeed some salts like MgCl<sub>2</sub> are chaotropic due to their size and ionization, causing disordering of organic layers [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-16'>16</a>]. Additionally, the presence of salt can desiccate a cell entirely if water molecules are trapped in the crystalline structure of a hydrated salt mineral [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-17'>17</a>]. Salts can also improve the availability of liquid water by depressing its freezing temperature, as is potentially crucial on the surface and in the near- and deep-subsurface of Mars [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-18'>18</a>].</p>

<h2>1.3 Salts and Analytical Chemistry</h2>

<p>Salts are a headache for many an analytical chemist, from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopists users to mass spectrometrists. In NMR, the issue with salts that it reduces signal strength, drowning the signal from elements and compounds with weaker intermolecular forces [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-19'>19</a>], decreasing the signal-to-noise (s/n) ratio. In mass spectrometry, the typical instrument of choice for environmental analysis of specific organics, the machine and its chemical separation components directly contact the salts, leading to issues with clogging and buildup of ions on components [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-20'>20</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-21'>21</a>]. Ionization, the step of making the compounds of interest available for sorting by the mass spectrometer, is impeded by the presence of salt, which preferentially ionizes. Adducts can also form, transient bonds between organics and salts which change the observed mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of the compound [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-22'>22</a>]. In all, detectability of trace organics becomes difficult as salt concentration increases.</p>

<p>A few solutions exist: first, removing the salt, second, diluting the sample, and third, using a method which is more robust to the salt [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-23'>23</a>]. In this work, we dilute our samples and analyze them using a more robust method. Desalting is avoided here due to its complexities in affecting sample integrity, be it in contamination, fractionation, or differential removal of organics [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-24'>24</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-25'>25</a>]. Promising work has been performed in the engineering of electrodialysis systems for the removal of salt without filtration. In the future, further analyses should be performed comparing salt removal systems, aimed at understanding their impacts on organic inventories [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-26'>26</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-27'>27</a>].</p>

<h2>1.4 Analytical Chemistry For Life Detection at Mars and Europa</h2>

<p>The only chemical life detection experiments performed on the surface of another planetary body were by the twin <em>Viking</em> landers on Mars in 1976 [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-28'>28</a>]. The results of these experiments has been deemed anywhere from positive, inconclusive, to negative by experts, with a consensus eventually reached by the community that they did not convincingly disprove that abiotic reactions could have caused the results [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-29'>29</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-30'>30</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-31'>31</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-32'>32</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-33'>33</a>]. The exploration of Mars in following decades focused on determining whether liquid water exists or has existed on its surface [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-34'>34</a>]. In the search for liquid water, the community began to understand the cause of the conflicting results from the<em> Viking</em> mission. By 2008, reactive perchlorates were discovered in the regolith thanks to electrochemical experiments by the<em> Phoenix</em> lander [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-35'>35</a>]. Evidence for present-day flowing water was discovered in the form of recurring slope lineae, and then possibly debunked [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-36'>36</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-37'>37</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-38'>38</a>].</p>

<p>Methane was detected in the Martian atmosphere by remote sensing, orbital measurements, and on the surface by the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL)<em> Curiosity</em> rover in such high concentration that perhaps it was biogenic [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-39'>39</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-40'>40</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-41'>41</a>]. However followup analyses of this methane by more sensitive orbiters presented conflicting data [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-42'>42</a>].</p>

<p>The<em> Curiosity</em> rover’s Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument then determined that organic compounds were present in the mudstones of a dry lake bed in Gale Crater, providing the first confirmation of complex organics (sulfur-rich aliphatic and aromatic compounds) present on Mars [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-43'>43</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-44'>44</a>]. Unfortunately, many SAM detections have been fraught with contamination due to the leak of the complex organic derivatization reagent <em>N-tert</em>- Butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (MTBSTFA) and the shedding of organics by the hydrocarbon trapping mechanism [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-45'>45</a>]. Thus, the search for more indigenous, intact organics on Mars, unmodified by pyrolysis or reaction with contaminants is ongoing. Images of hexagonal desiccation cracks and analyses by<em> Curiosity</em>’s laser induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) instrument ChemCam have indicated that salts are present in this lake bed among these clays [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-46'>46</a>]. This suggests wet-dry cycling of the lake was occurring before its disappearance [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-47'>47</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-48'>48</a>]. This, and other evidence, has solidified the notion that Gale Crater was at one point habitable and perhaps even “urable,” able to support an origin of life [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-49'>49</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-50'>50</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-51'>51</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-52'>52</a>]. SAM analyses of carbon isotopes of methane released by the pyrolysis of regolith samples from Gale Crater indicate isotopic depletion, though with high variation. This has been attributed to either photolysis of biologically produced methane, photoreduction of atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub>, or deposition of cosmic dust from a galactic cloud [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-53'>53</a>].</p>

<p>Since this point, the recent<em> Perseverance</em> rover, tasked with selecting samples for return by the Mars Sample Return (MSR) program has discovered the Cheyava Falls target, a rock with patterns of spots which indicate potential oxidation and reduction reactions in taking place in the mineral matrix [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-54'>54</a>]. The spots were claimed by the mission team to be “potential biosignatures” that cannot be assessed fully until they are returned to Earth. The rover has two instruments which have the potential to assess biosignatures, Planetary Instrument for X-ray Lithochemistry (PIXL) and Scanning Habitable Environments with Raman and Luminescence for Organics and Chemicals (SHERLOC), which intend to map elemental and organic spatial distributions respectively [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-55'>55</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-56'>56</a>]. However, the SHERLOC’s spectral detection methods are naturally ambiguous when Martian samples can hold unexpected interferences. Any organic compound detections by this instrument may in fact be attributed to cesium ions in igneous materials, a fact realized after the mission had already announced detection of nonspecific organics using spectral means such as ultraviolet (UV) and Raman spectroscopy [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-57'>57</a>].</p>

<p>Mars holds great potential for life detection, but the process is slow and halting due to analytical difficulties. Until the arrival of the <em>Rosalind</em> <em>Franklin</em> rover, containing a more advanced mass spectrometer with a laser desorption ionization mode, or until the return of the NASA/ESA Mars Sample Return or the CNSA<em> Tianwen-3</em> sample return mission’s samples to Earth, we are unlikely to have an answer.</p>

<p>No life detection experiments have been performed on liquid samples from another planetary body. Europa is believed to have a global subsurface ocean and salts on its surface [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-58'>58</a>]. Upwelling beneath this icy surfaces may bring water from below, concentrate it, and erupt it onto the surface [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-59'>59</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-60'>60</a>]. Interests also lie with Enceladus, a moon of Saturn with plumes of organic- and salt-rich water being ejected from its south pole [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-12'>12</a>]. If organics are present in these oceans below, scientists believe they could be concentrated by these processes to bring them to the surface [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-61'>61</a>]. Missions have been designed to detect these organics such as the <em>Orbilander</em> concept suggested by the National Academies planetary science decadal survey, which must be equipped with technology robust enough to operate effectively after a decade long transit [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-62'>62</a>]. In the case of Europa, the presence of plumes are less certain, but the presence of salts visible on the surface. Its likely that if organics are on Europa’s surface, they will be among salts. For Enceladus, the organics are certain and they may be easily accessible from orbit [seaton2023astrobiology]. Some of these ejected organics are associated with sodium, suggesting that salt may also play a role in our analyses of Enceladan material [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-63'>63</a>].</p>

<p>A once planned mission, the <em>Europa</em> <em>Lander</em>, had ambitions of landing on the body’s surface and sampling the ice or snow for these organics and potentially detecting life [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-64'>64</a>]. However, much more is still to be learned about Europa before a landed mission, first by the upcoming NASA-led<em> Europa Clipper</em> mission, which will orbit Jupiter and study its icy moon, along with the ESA-led<em> Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer JUICE</em> mission [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-65'>65</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-66'>66</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-67'>67</a>].</p>

<p>What Europa and Mars (and possibly Enceladus) have in common are their high concentrations of surface salt and the likelihood that salt will be co-located with any organic biosignatures. Therefore, our missions to these surfaces should be equipped with analytical tools capable of detecting organic compounds among salts, either in liquid or solid samples.</p>

<h2>1.5 Analog Studies and the Oceans Across Space and Time team</h2>

<p>In order to directly learn about the organic chemistry and presence of life on other worlds we must deploy either: optical remote-sensing, data-transmitting surface instrumentation, atmospheric sensing, or sample-return campaigns. Often, as is the case of Earth’s Moon, Mars, asteroids Bennu and Ryugu, comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko, Venus, and Titan, a combination of two, three, or even all four strategies are used to perform these analyses. In some cases, such as for Enceladus and possibly Europa, the analysis of plumes (carrying the materials of interest to space) from orbit or fly-by missions is also an option, as is combining<em> in situ</em> analysis techniques like mass spectrometry with orbital fly-by architectures [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-68'>68</a>].</p>

<p>Another option exists for learning about other worlds. By using a combination of properties learned on Earth, either in the lab or in a field site, and of properties of the planetary body of interest, we can make inferences and hypothesize models which may be tested by future missions [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-69'>69</a>]. In this thesis we use places where certain properties of other planetary bodies exist on Earth, known as analog sites. Such work examines Earth to learn about how things may be on other worlds. Perhaps the earliest planetary analog sites existed for the Apollo astronauts, training them to identify geology on the lunar surface and testing their equipment in natural basaltic environments [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-70'>70</a>]. Today, the planetary science community, both engineers and scientists, uses analog sites to test their systems and to learn about natural processes.</p>

<p>The Oceans Across Space and Time (OAST) team is a NASA-funded consortium of biologists, chemists, and geologists from universities across the United States. The team has three primary analog field sites for full team deployment: the South Bay salt works (SBSW) in Chula Vista, California, the hypersaline, acidic lakes of Western Australia (WA), and the deep hypersaline anoxic Orca Basin in the Gulf of Mexico. This thesis primarily reports on work from SBSW and WA, while the appendix includes preliminary results from Orca Basin. OAST approaches field sites as life detection missions in themselves. When exploring hypersaline regions near the limit of life, its crucial to understand the geochemical limits to habitability, the biological mechanisms and population present there, and the chemical biosignatures and signs of activity which are detectable. The work in this thesis falls into the third category. In the analysis of brine for amino acids, key biosignature molecules, we push the boundaries of what is analytically feasible, using capillary electrophoresis (CE) to separate them in hypersaline mixtures. By detecting adenosine triphosphate (ATP) at these sites we look for signs of microbial activity and learn how this biomolecule may or may not best represent microbial activity in stressful environments. We also see it as an example of the preservation and evapoconcentration of organics. In solid samples we use<em> Curiosity</em> rover-like analysis techniques to understand the impact of the mineral matrix on detectability of another class of biosignature molecule, fatty acids. These topics which comprise this thesis will, in due time, all be contextualized in the literature within the larger characterization of each field site by dozens of teammates.</p>

<p>Chapter 2 details a method for capillary electrophoresis-laser induced fluorescence (CE-LIF) separation of amino acids in liquid samples which is also used in later chapters of this thesis. This includes the quantification of some amino acids in a hypersaline sample from the SBSW. Chapter 3 reports on the scientific findings when this CE-LIF method is used to quantify more amino acids in brine samples from a series of ponds at the SBSW, along with the results of a bioluminescence based ATP assay. Targeted analysis of the brine is also performed with microchip capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry (µCE-MS).</p>

<p>Chapter 4 describes this same analysis suite performed at a series of lakes in Western Australia spanning a range of acidity and hypersalinity. These lakes at the SBSW and in Western Australia are in some ways analogs of ancient Mars and potentially present day Europa, where chemical fingerprints of potential life may be changing in response to the polyextremes of pH and hypersalinity.</p>

<p>Chapter 5 is an analysis of solid samples from three of the Western Australian lakes using TMAH thermochemolysis, a method included on the<em> Curiosity</em> rover and the upcoming <em>Rosalind</em> <em>Franklin</em> rover. These samples included a halite salt crust, a gypsum salt crust, and aluminum-rich clays. A comparison is performed between these modern samples and samples from the hyperarid Yungay region of Chile’s Atacama desert which represent an ancient, postdepositionally altered endmember.</p>

<p>Chapter 6 provides perspectives from my time applying analytical chemistry to the problems of life detection and describes two novel approaches for biosignature science, of exploring chemical space in an nontargeted fashion, and of observing antifragile (i.e. stressor adaptive) behavior in systems.</p>
