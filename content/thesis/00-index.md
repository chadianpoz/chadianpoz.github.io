## Summary

The search for signs of life in our solar system today in many ways comes down to the capabilities of analytical chemical instrumentation. This thesis primarily explores the impact of salts on analytical measurements of biosignature molecules in a series of analog sites for Mars and Europa. We study small biological organic molecules such as amino acids, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and fatty acids. Observing these on Earth will help us understand the provenance, relative concentrations, and detectability of organic compounds on planetary surfaces when they are measured by analytical tools <em>in</em> <em>situ</em> or in returned samples.

We determined that the concentrations of these small organic compounds, even those which typically undergo rapid degradation, like ATP, increase in sites undergoing evapoconcentration processes. The increase in salt concentration and decrease in water availability stresses the extant microbes to release high concentrations of osmolyte compounds. These osmolyte compounds and the amino acid residues of salt-adapted proteins are characteristic of biological adaptation to these conditions, making them robust biosignatures. Capillary electrophoresis paired with laser induced fluorescence (CE-LIF) is a powerful tool for the detection of these biosignatures in hypersaline and acidic liquid samples. Evapoconcentrative systems often generate solid salt crusts. Molecular biosignature patterns from the microbial population of the overlying brine are entrapped and preserved within salt crusts over geologic time, making biosignatures particularly detectable in CaSO<sub>2</sub> with thermochemolysis.
