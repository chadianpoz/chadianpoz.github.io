<h2>4.1 Introduction</h2>

<p>In the search for extraterrestrial life on Mars, Europa, and Enceladus, three planetary bodies thought to have some characteristics of habitability in their past or present, the life discovered is likely be microbial [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-210'>210</a>] [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-211'>211</a>] [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-212'>212</a>]. Life in these environments would be exposed to stressors such as extreme temperature, UV and energetic particle radiation, pH, salinity, and in the case of Mars and the salty regions of Europa’s surface, lack of available water molecules [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-80'>80</a>]. As we know from observations of life on Earth, these extremes may necessitate special adaptations in the lifeform’s chemical machinery which allow it to metabolize and reproduce in that particular environment [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-213'>213</a>]. In this study, we approach a polyextreme environment at the intersection of hypersalinity and acidity, and build an understanding of the effects of those properties on organic chemical biosignatures. The purpose is twofold: first, to explore the changing chemical fingerprint across the two-dimensions of salinity and pH extremes as a biosignature of adaptation, and second, to further evaluate the efficacy of CE-LIF and µCE-MS as strategies for life detection and environmental analytical chemistry on Earth and on other worlds.</p>

<p>The acid brine lakes of Western Australia are a diverse set of ephemeral, wet-dry cycling ponds which exist across a number of paleovalleys in the southwest of the state [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-214'>214</a>][<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-215'>215</a>]. The combination of acidity and hypersalinity exhibited by some of these lakes drives life to diversify in adaptation to these extremes [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-216'>216</a>]. These microbes often utilize specialized salt- and acidity- resistant proteins and the accumulation of osmolyte compounds to counteract the osmotic stress [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-217'>217</a>]. The Oceans Across Space and Time (OAST) team’s field season in August 2022 included analyses of more than 30 lake systems across Western Australia [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-215'>215</a>]. Lakes in this region have been the subject of previous work relating them to ancient Mars, as their wet-dry cycling mirrors what results from the Curiosity rover indicate happened billions of years ago on the red planet [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-218'>218</a>]. While work performed at the South Bay salt works in Chula Vista, CA (SBSW) by the OAST team in 2019 and 2020 is similar in its study of hypersaline and acidic systems, those salt works ponds are manipulated to evaporate water and concentrate salt [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-219'>219</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-112'>112</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>]. Therefore, the salt works are an artificial analog of Martian evapoconcentration, with less analogous wet-dry cycling. In our study, the acid brine lakes of WA are a more natural analog of Martian wet-dry cycling.</p>

<p>We therefore study organic biosignatures in this analog site to enable life detection on planetary bodies where evaporites and wet-dry cycling, salt-deposits, and brines generally occur. From a biosignature perspective, we not only expect to observe biosignature compounds themselves, but we also hypothesize that we will observe a shift in the pattern of expressed proteins and in the presence of osmolyte metabolites among these lakes to represent adaptation to these extreme environments. However, not only the biology is at play here. The taphonomy (i.e., preservation) of the organics along with the analytical limitations and biases of our methods are also factors in shifting the window of observable compounds [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-221'>221</a>].</p>

<p>In the detection of life on other worlds, only limited samples and limited analysis suites are available [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-222'>222</a>]. Liquid samples from another planetary body have never been analyzed [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-223'>223</a>]. However, in our future exploration of the surfaces and subsurfaces of ocean worlds like Europa and Enceladus, and of Mars, which may have subsurface liquid water, we need techniques which can analyze liquids [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-224'>224</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-225'>225</a>]. In this work, we use a separation method paired separately with two detection methods, each of which may be capable of the detection of microbial life on planetary bodies. Capillary electrophoresis (CE) is a promising separation method for use in the exploration of aqueous samples from hypersaline systems [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-226'>226</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-185'>185</a>]. When paired with the low limits of detection of sample derivatization followed by laser induced fluorescence (LIF) detection it has been used to probe acidic, basic, and hypersaline liquids [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-227'>227</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-89'>89</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-228'>228</a>]. Other techniques such as mass spectrometry (MS) are hindered by salt ions, and can in fact be damaged by them [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-21'>21</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-20'>20</a>]. While much can be done to desalt or otherwise filter the organics from these ions, this will often lead to unintended fractionation effects on the organic pool or at least a reduction in the overall DOC [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-27'>27</a>]. MS, used often in spaceflight but never before interfaced for liquid handling on a planetary mission, is tested in this work with microchip capillary electrophoresis (µCE).</p>

<p>Here we analyze the organic, polar (aqueously soluble) small molecules present across 29 samples from 26 lakes in Western Australia that span a wide range of pH and salinity. We use CE-LIF to quantify primary amines present and we include the use of µCE-MS analysis of a less specific set of polar compounds of two lake waters. We also use a bioluminescence assay to measure ATP concentration, nominally a tracer of microbial activity [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-229'>229</a>] in 36 samples.</p>

<h2>4.2 Methods</h2>

<p>All reagents were used as received except where otherwise noted.</p>

<h3>4.2.1 Sample collection</h3>

<p>Lake samples (Appendix Table C.1) were collected through a sample-rinsed tube attached to a peristaltic pump extended by a 2 m aluminum pole in order to collect away from the edge of ponds and away from where the team was standing in the water. Water was collected directly into two 15 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes. Blanks were collected by pouring 18 MΩMilliQ-filtered water carried from Georgia Tech into new tubes at the sampling site. One tube was stored at room temperature (RT) until analysis by ATP assay within 24-48 hours. The other tube was immediately put on dry ice and then transported back to Georgia Tech for long term storage at -80°C until analysis by CE-LIF and µCE-MS.</p>

<h3>4.2.2 CE-LIF analyses of amino acids</h3>

<p>CE-LIF analysis of lake water samples was performed as described previously with the same reagents and instrumentation [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-227'>227</a>]. Briefly, lake samples were thawed from storage at -80°C at room temperature. Lake water (10 µL) was added to 89 µL of sodium tetraborate run buffer at a concentration of 70 mM. Then, 1 µL of carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dissolved in DMF at a concentration of 2 mM was added before vortexing and storage in darkness at room temperature for approximately 24 hours to react. This process was performed in triplicate, and each sample was analyzed individually by CE-LIF using an autosampler and hydrodynamic injection of each sample at 0.4 psi for 5 seconds. The system used was a SciEx PACE MDQ with a laser induced fluorescence module exciting with a 488 nM laser. Samples were separated in a 60 cm fused-silica capillary with a 30 kV potential across a run buffer of 70 mM sodium tetraborate. Each analysis of neutral and basic amino acids lasted 45 minutes.</p>

<p>Data was then reduced from ASCII format to CSV using an in-house python script, resulting in signal vs time data at a rate of 4 Hz. Electropherograms were then plotted and compared individually by eye to identify shifts in electroosmotic flow and stretch them accordingly. Then, peaks were identified from standard addition experiments where individual compounds eluted. Lake samples electropherograms were then annotated with amino acid identities and amplitude was calculated, using a separate in-house python-based GUI.</p>

<h3>4.2.3 µCE-MS</h3>

<p>Analysis by µCE-MS was performed identically as described by Pozarycki et al., (2024) on WA water samples using the ZipChip system from 908 Devices (Boston, MA) interfaced with the QExactive Orbitrap MS from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA) [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>]. A buffer known as the background electrolyte was used for separation which was composed of 68% water, 20% isopropanol, 10% acetonitrile, and 2% formic acid. A solution for diluting the samples was 80% methanol, 20% water, and<em> &lt;</em>1% ammonium acetate. Before use, the buffer and diluent were acidified adding a 50% mixture of water and formic acid by volume. Water filtered by a MilliQ system at 18 MΩalong with HPLC grade solvents from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, MA) were used. Lake water was diluted 1:4 in diluent and 4 nL was injected for analysis. Mass spectrometer settings were at 17,500 m/Dm at 500 m/z with full-scans at a rate of 12 Hz. Collection was performed with Xcalibur 3.0 software and compounds were identified via a target list in Skyline software [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-176'>176</a>].</p>

<h3>4.2.4 ATP bioluminescence assay</h3>

<p>The ATP bioluminescence assay was performed using HSII kits from Roche Diagnostics (Basel, Switzerland) with the method as described in Pozarycki et al 2024 and elsewhere [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-230'>230</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-231'>231</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-232'>232</a>]. Experiments on samples were performed near-field in non-laboratory accommodations in Western Australia within 48 hours of sample collection in 2022. Experiments to assess attenuation of signal were performed in a lab setting in 2023. Briefly, 1000 mL of lake water was added to 1000 mL of 100 mM Tris and 4 mM EDTA buffer before heating in boiling water for 3 minutes and cooling in room temperature water for 3 minutes. A salt interference dilution experiment was performed with a range of lake water samples before analysis with 50 µL of sample to 200 µL of buffer chosen to minimize salt interference while maintaining a high enough ATP concentration to be detectable.</p>

<h3>4.2.5 Statistical methods</h3>

<p>A CSV file containing geochemical parameters, blank-subtracted and sum-normalized CE- LIF peak amplitude measurements, and ATP concentrations with their RSVs was loaded into R (v4.3.1). Pairwise correlation matrices, filtered by significance (p-value <em>&lt;</em> 0.05), were generated to identify statistically relevant relationships. Correlation matrix, p-value, and bubble plots were generated in R using ggplot2, ggally, corrplot, and dplyr. For PCA, missing values were imputed using the median of each parameter (zeros remained). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the FactoMineR package (v2.7) on standardized data (mean centered and scaled to unit variance). The first two principal components, explaining the greatest variance, were selected to visualize relationships among samples and to identify drivers of separation. Scatterplots were generated using the ggplot2 and ggrepel packages.</p>

<h3>4.3.1 Sample site</h3>

<p>Thirty-four samples were collected from 26 lakes in southwest Western Australia. These lakes are a part of 5 major paleovalleys and therefore represent differing overlying geology (Plattner et al. <em>in</em> <em>prep</em>). Lakes with a range of pH and <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> were targeted to gain an understanding of the effect of these factors on the biology present and their chemical biosignatures. Samples in this work were collected by three teams, a ”fast” and ”slow” team (denoted F and S in figures). The former performed a standard set of analyses at each lake, with some lakes revisited by the latter team who performed more time-intensive analyses. A ”bonus” team (denoted B) was deployed to the Westernmost sites after the primary field work was completed. Because a more limited set of geochemical analyses was performed at these sites, samples from this team were not included in multivariate statistical analyses below. Further work was performed by the OAST team in January 2024 (denoted N) which was not incorporated into this study.</p>

<h3>4.3.2 Separation and quantification of amino acids in lake water</h3>

<figure><img src='/thesis/04-03.webp' alt='Figure 4.2: Capillary electrophoresis-laser induced fluorescence (CE-LIF) separation of a sample of lake water from Lake Gounter in Western Australia (Sample number T37, site F8 S) diluted 1:10 in 70 ' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Figure 4.2: Capillary electrophoresis-laser induced fluorescence (CE-LIF) separation of a sample of lake water from Lake Gounter in Western Australia (Sample number T37, site F8 S) diluted 1:10 in 70 mM sodium tetraborate buffer prior to derivatization. Analysis with lower buffer concentration can be used to detect the amino acids Glu and Asp.</figcaption></figure>

<p>Separation and relative quantification of amino acids via CE-LIF was performed successfully with a 1:10 dilution of sample to buffer (Figure 4.2). This demonstrates the efficacy of the method used in Seaton et al., (2023) and Pozarycki et al., (2024) on two disparate hypersaline regions, one a human-made saltern fed by ocean water and the other naturallyoccurring inland lake systems, thousands of miles apart. Peak identification of this sample yielded slightly different results from the separation in previous work. This may be a result of matrix induced changes to the separation efficiency, to interactions between the compounds, or to misidentification in the previous work, highlighting the difficulties of CE-LIF peak identification relative to multi-dimensional methods like µCE-MS also used in this study. At the time of writing, preliminary analysis has been performed on these samples with relative quantification based on amplitude, rather than area under the curve. It is likely that both amplitude and area under the curve will prove effective in absolute quantification, though some percent error in the accuracy of amplitude relative to area can likely be attributed to peak broadening. A number of unidentified peaks are present in the electropherogram of each sample in the same region as the amino acids which have been identified. Work to identify these using standards, likely other metabolites such as osmolytes and polypeptides, is underway. Furthermore, separation using a buffer of lower concentration allows the quantitation of Glu and Asp, as the amino acids are most acidic in solution.</p>

<h3>4.3.3 µCE-MS of lake waters</h3>

<p>For discovery of a more generalized set of small polar compounds in the WA water samples µCE-MS was used as the CE-LIF method used here is only used for primary amines. The high resolution of the method enables identification of these compounds based on a target list, which provides exact masses of the [M+H] ion. Lakes Brown and Gounter were first analyzed via µCE-MS. Lake Brown contained higher relative concentrations of amino acids proline and valine, as well as osmolyte glycine betaine, and lower concentrations of amino acids serine, leucine, asparagine, glutamine, lysine, glutamic acid, arginine, osmolyte ornithine, and polypeptide glycylglycine. Analysis revealed no detected compounds in Lake Gounter using the same approach. At time of each collection Lake Brown had an <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> of 0.89 and a Na<sup>+</sup> concentration of 4.9 M, while Lake Gounter had an<em> a</em><sub>w</sub> of 0.94 and a Na<sup>+</sup> concentration of 3.2 M.</p>

<h3>4.3.4 ATP in lake water and solid samples</h3>

<figure><img src='/thesis/04-05.webp' alt='Figure 4.3: ATP concentration in each lake represented as circle/square size, plotted against pH and water activity. Red squares represent samples collected by the first team, while blue circles repre' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Figure 4.3: ATP concentration in each lake represented as circle/square size, plotted against pH and water activity. <p>Changes in pH and <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> (Figure 4.2) across sampling days does not affect any lakes ATP concentration other than F9 and F10, Twin Lake West and Twin Lake East, lakes adjacent to each other physically separated by a roadway. ATP concentration decreases (on the order of 1 pg/g or less, but greater than their RSD) with a slight decrease in pH (less than half a point). These lakes have a halite crust which is studied in detail in Chapter 5.</p>

<p>ATP concentrations in the WA acid brine lakes (Figure 4.2) were higher in samples with pH greater than 6. Surprisingly, it does not vary with <em>a</em><sub>w</sub>, (and therefore with salt concentration and ionic strength). This is opposite the trend observed at SBSW, where pH and <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> decrease was associated with a 50x increase in ATP concentration. WA has lakes which inhabit all ”four corners” of the pH and ATP relationship with a greater range of acidity, down to below pH 3, than SBSW which neared pH 6. However, WA has a smaller range of<em> a</em><sub>w</sub>, not reaching below 0.70 during our austral winter 2022 season, unlike SBSW where<em> a</em><sub>w</sub> reached below 0.40 for the most Mg<sup>2+</sup>-rich sites.</p>

<p>Despite our comments on ATP assay attenuation below, which suggest that ATP concentration should not be used as a tracer of activity in saline samples, a similar trend of markedly decreased activity below pH 6 is observed by the more robust activity assays performed in Paris et al. <em>in</em> <em>prep</em> and Paris and Arandia-Gorostidi et al. 2024 [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-188'>188</a>] between SBSW and the WA acid brine lakes. In that work, analyses of the cellular uptake of isotopically labeled amino acids provide evidence that cellular metabolism is occuring in the majority of lakes with pH greater than 6, while below this threshold it does not. This trend is shown to hold regardless of <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> within the range of 1.00 to 0.70. Similarly, DNA analyses by Klempay et al. <em>in prep</em> on the samples of water from WA indicates that a cutoff in microbial diversity exists at pH 3.</p>

<h3>4.3.5 ATP assay attenuation</h3>

<figure><img src='/thesis/04-06.webp' alt='Figure 4.4: A plot of ATP signal via bioluminescence assay vs increasing salt concentration in laboratory experiments to determine the signal attenuation caused to the assay.' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Figure 4.4: A plot of ATP signal via bioluminescence assay vs increasing salt concentration in laboratory experiments to determine the signal attenuation caused to the assay.</figcaption></figure>

<p>The ATP bioluminescence assay was performed on solutions of pure NaCl and MgCl<sub>2</sub> salt in MilliQ-filtered water in concentrations ranging from 0.1 M to 3 M (Fig 4.3). Analysis was performed on ATP aliquots in water with concentrations ranging from 10<sup>-13</sup> to 10<sup>-8</sup> M. Luminescence decreased uniformly with concentration of ATP, where only 10<sup>-8</sup> M showed high enough RLU to be reasonably quantified over the salt range used. Over salt concentration, the greatest attenuation occurred between 0.1 and 0.5 M of either salt. We did not measure the attenuation between 0 M and 0.1 M, but previous standard curves of ATP in pure water suggest a signal of 50,000 RLU is typical for this concentration. This, and the difference between the NaCl and MgCl<sub>2</sub> RLU value at 0.1 M suggests that the latter salt has an earlier drop-off in signal with concentration relative to NaCl. This demonstrates that in natural samples with concentrations of salt of 0.5 M or higher the detected ATP signal is likely decreased by approximately 100x. However, in previous work analyzing ATP in brine at at the South Bay Salt Works (SBSW) saltern we observed RLU signal corresponding to tens of nanomolar (10<sup>-8</sup> M) ATP concentrations even in brines with concentrations of both Mg<sup>2+</sup> and Na<sup>+</sup> near 3 M. Assuming no other analytically relevant factors, in order for ATP to be detectable by this assay at these levels of salt interference the actual concentration of ATP must at least be at micromolar (10<sup>-6</sup> M) levels. Therefore, given our detection of ATP in the WA acid brine lakes and similarly to our description of behavior at SBSW ATP is concentrated by evaporation processes in these seasonally wet-dry cycling lakes and ATP is preserved for longer timescales than is expected in less saline environmental samples, allowing it to be concentrated. The preservation may be due to less active uptake and protein-mediated hydrolysis of the valuable energy molecule by other scavenging cells and/or due to the stabilization of the molecular structure against abiotic hydrolytic breakdown by cations like Mg<sup>2+</sup>. Due to this extreme salt attenuation in the assay and as well as these three environmental factors, ATP is not an effective tracer of cellular activity near the salinity limits of life.</p>

<p>Boron, phosphate, and pH are the only geochemical parameters with statistically significant correlations with ATP concentration, all which are positive (Fig 4.5). pH is the most interesting, indicating a potential chemical or biological phenomenon induced by the pH, as discussed in the section above. The amino acid relative abundances here have no statistically significant correlations with any parameter other than each other.</p>

<p>PCA of all geochemical parameters and organics shows two major groupings (Fig 4.6) associated with near neutral pH and high <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> and low pH and low <em>a</em><sub>w</sub>. Separation in the first principal component (PC1) accounts for 28.2% of the variance and is driven to the left primarily by dissolved oxygen (DO), <em>a</em><sub>w</sub>, and pH, and to the right by salinity parameters such as total dissolved solids (TDS), conductivity, and sodium and chloride concentrations (Fig 4.6b). The second principal component (PC2) accounts for 14.6% of variance and is primarily driven by boron, dissolved organic carbon, and bromine, a known marker of evapoconcentration [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-220'>220</a>]. Notably, ATP is also directly contributory to PC2, though to a lesser degree.</p>

<p>Initially we hypothesized that this clear distinction is between those lakes which have the most evapoconcentration, and thus the most buildup of organics. Indeed, F9, F10, S1, and many other lakes with low <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> all had salt crusts under their surface while many of the lakes with higher <em>a</em><sub>w</sub> did not. However the absence of a large separation in the groupings along PC2 driven by DOC and bromine, evapoconcentrative tracers, and their lack of statistcally significant correlation with<em> a</em><sub>w</sub> and pH (Fig 4.5) suggests that<em> a</em><sub>w</sub> and pH are not strong indicators of evapoconcentration.</p>

<p>Changes in geochemical space between collection dates are most pronounced in lakes F13 (Warden), F14 (Pink), F9 (Twin West), and F8 (Gounter). Movement is generally towards one region of the plot over sampling time where pH and<em> a</em><sub>w</sub> do not change greatly. This may be due to other contributing variables in the PCA, but could also be due in part to the imputation used to fill gaps in geochemical data not collected between the first and second collection of samples. In astrobiological studies, and environmental studies generally, gaps in geochemical metadata data can significantly hinder interpretation of meta-analyses [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-233'>233</a>].</p>

<h2>4.4 Next Steps</h2>

<p>Further analysis of these samples should proceed for absolute quantification of amino acids (including glutamic and aspartic acid) and of any osmolytes or polypeptides identifiable. Re-analysis of ATP in samples from WA, both stored at -80°C and at room temperature, has the capacity to shed light on the preservation of ATP in these samples, assuming no active microbial activity in stored samples. Along with this, room temperature samples from SBSW should be re-analyzed nearly 5 years later for the same reasons. µCE-MS is difficult to perform with brine samples, suggesting that better luck may be had with more dilute, or desalted samples, however the risks of biasing a sample’s organic compound distribution seems high. Studies on organic compound fractionation in cation-exchange resin columns as compared to electrodialysis methods should be performed [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-26'>26</a>], and can be visualized by excitation-emission plots which provide a snapshot of organic chemical space. Analyses of lake waters via LC-MS can be attempted to directly compare CE-LIF and µCE-MS capabilities in dealing with matrix effects, however it should be noted that damage to instrument may occur, obscuring results.</p>

<p>Studies have been performed on the extraction of amino acids from microbial samples under varying pH that indicate that the recovery of certain amino acids is affected by pH, though the methods used in our work are not analogous to those in the mentioned pH study [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-234'>234</a>]). Therefore, work could be envisioned which changes the pH of the samples via buffering to neutral before analysis. However, our use of a dilution buffer, sodium tetraborate, likely has this effect, though the method has not been systematically tested for pH tolerance. Salt tolerance has also not been explicitly tested with this method and should be done to get a full picture of the analytical biases which may be introduced by the presence of salt. To this point, the effective detection of biosignature molecules has been effective evidence of a functioning method, but more in depth tests would be enlightening.</p>
