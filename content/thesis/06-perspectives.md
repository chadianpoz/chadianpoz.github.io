<h2>6.1 The Need For Life Detection Science</h2>

<p>The work in this thesis uses analytical chemistry tools to detect organic compounds in hypersaline planetary analog sites. However, many assumptions underlie the experiments and analyses which were performed. In this section, we will outline some of these assumptions which have not already been mentioned in the introductory chapter, and suggest further work with these nuances in mind.</p>

<p>When performing life detection experiments on Earth in the context of astrobiology, even in extreme environments, we are working with an underlying hypothesis that these planetary analog sites will have signs of life present in some capacity, either from extant or extinct life. Often in sites where the limit to life is reached, evidence of life is found nearby just below that threshold, and often it is ambiguous whether any life at all exists beyond that threshold due to analytical failings or due to preservation of biomolecules [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-233'>233</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-291'>291</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-16'>16</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-117'>117</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-292'>292</a>].</p>

<p>A common operational goal for analog testing of instrumentation is to see the instruments detect that expected sign of life on Earth [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-293'>293</a>]. In this thesis the hypersaline and acidic nature of the liquid matrix was the first challenge which was to be overcome, and was successfully managed using CE-LIF and a 10x sample dilution. µCE-MS had some success with challenges in repeatability because salt clogged the microchip tip and caused the nanoESI spray to fail. Further testing with dedicated methods for hypersaline analysis are still necessary. The ATP assay was not being used as a biosignature detection method analogous to what would be used in spaceflight, but was to understand the systems being studied and how cellular activity changes and can be measured in these environments. The assay was substantially attenuated due to high salt concentration. The ATP assay was a particularly good example of both matrix and preservation effects causing impacts which could not be disentangled.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, a number of morphological structures and textures, isotope ratios, chemical patterns, and biologically associated minerals can be produced by abiotic processes where strong chemical energy gradients exist [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-294'>294</a>]. In fact, these false biosignatures are more likely to occur in those places where we suspect life may be present. Those areas with dynamic water-rock processes and those which produce gradients of chemical energy in the presence of abundant organic compounds all have the capability to produce a multitude of false biosignatures [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-294'>294</a>]. In particular, this seems to be an issue with preserved biosignatures, as opposed to those being actively generated in the environment. Degradation of a strong biosignature may cause it to appear an ambiguous biosignature, explainable by abiotic processes.</p>

<p>Separate from the analytical objectives, an understanding of “biosignature science” at these sites is also a crucial outcome of our work. We aimed to understand how compounds and their patterns are enriched in concentration, changed in expression, preserved, or destroyed by the properties of the analog site.</p>

<p>A number of current and upcoming missions have potential to detect biosignatures on other planetary bodies, <em>in</em> <em>situ</em> (Table 6.1). While more missions are likely to occur in this and the next decade than ever before for life detection, behavioral biosignatures, such as those metabolic observations by the<em> Viking</em> missions are not being strongly considered. Perhaps the most powerful impetus for bringing analytical mass to the surface of Mars and for scientific exploration of Mars generally will come with crewed spaceflight. While these missions will be the most expensive missions to Mars they will provide the political and economic will to catalyze life detection science and astrobiology generally.</p>

<p>The individual missions with the greatest singular chance for immediate life detection in our solar system may be behind us. If a global, highly visible biosphere similar to that on Earth, was present on Mars or any other planetary body it would have been observable through the remote sensing already performed by the <em>Mariner</em>, <em>Viking</em>, <em>Galileo</em>, <em>Cassini</em>, and<em> Voyager</em> spacecraft [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-293'>293</a>]. They revealed decades ago that plant or animal life did not cover the surfaces of any other planet in our solar system (sans Pluto, which was finally observed up close in 2015 by <em>New</em> <em>Horizons</em>). Indeed, analysis of atmospheres, or lack thereof, can provide us some confidence in this (perplexing methane measurements on Mars notwithstanding). After remote sensing, astrobiology was then reborn through wet chemistry in the<em> Viking</em> experiments [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-297'>297</a>], and as they showed through inconclusive results, it is not a straightforward science [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-33'>33</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-28'>28</a>]. Thus the search for trace biosignatures has now become more complex, ambiguous, and expensive. Theoretically, moons with obscuring clouds and ice shells may still hide signs of global biological activity, and thriving extant life may still be hidden meters below the surface of Mars [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-222'>222</a>].</p>

<p>Today, we have only a limited set of flown or near-mission ready tools for life detection [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-225'>225</a>]. Institutional incentive structures may not be effectively constructed to enable rapid innovation in this space, with great engineering potential from commercial partnerships for planetary science instrumentation not tapped since the<em> Viking</em> days, when Biospherics Inc helped provide the Labeled Release experiment. As NASA’s dependence on new commercial partners from startups like Intuitive Machines Inc. to Space Exploration Technologies Corp. grows, astrobiology will be given the opportunity to allow commercial entities into the fold [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-298'>298</a>], as the space biology discipline has been doing for decades to make experiments on the ISS low cost and frequent. Competitive mission formats have proven effective for New Frontiers class planetary missions up to now, but larger scale missions face larger complications, for which a more efficient incentive structure may someday be devised through public-private partnership.</p>

<p>Today, the greatest potential for life thriving outside of Earth (independent of human activity) exists on Mars, Enceladus, and Europa. Further sites for inspection include aerosol microbes hiding out since habitable days in Venus’ clouds, psychrohalophiles in Ceres’ possibly organic-rich subsurface brines, and non-water-based life on the bizarre surface of the nitrogen-organic-world Titan [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-299'>299</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-13'>13</a>]. Indeed, a good argument exists to search harder for life outside our solar system given the enormous number of planets in our galaxy. This gamblers-odds approach is enticing, but the quality and dimensionality of data (entirely spectral) which we can gain from a planet orbiting another star is demoralizing [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-300'>300</a>]. Alternative to atmospheric spectra, the search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) posits that loudmouth aliens would be the easiest to find [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-301'>301</a>]. Thus, the search for life can cross from disciplines of geology, to chemistry, to astronomy. Upcoming planetary science missions hold some potential for life detection, but persistent delays have caused dismay in the community.</p>

<p>Despite all this, biosignature science is in need of rethinking given the potential for false positives and ambiguous results that come with necessarily limited planetary science missions. Until samples are returned from other planetary bodies it may be impossible to directly observe enough chemical or micromorphological biosignatures to convince the scientific community completely of the presence of life on another planet. Possible false positives in the past between ALH84001 and the <em>Viking</em> experiments have demonstrated that even biosignatures that were assuredly thought to be signs of life at the time turned out to be easily replicated through abiotic means upon the need for thorough analysis [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-302'>302</a>]. That is, it is unlikely that a suggested biosignature or even a suite of biosignatures will be a “silver bullet,” as more resources will inevitably be put into disproving the biosignature after its observation on another planetary body [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-303'>303</a>].</p>

<p>While many others have philosophized on biosignatures and life detection strategies [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-304'>304</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-305'>305</a>] I will do so here using a new framework and may inevitably reach similar conclusions. Nonetheless, I must mention that I will not cover all important topics here but merely a few novel framings of familiar concepts.</p>

<h2>6.2 Loosening Our Definition of Life</h2>

<p>While this thesis focuses on the detection of chemical life, that which we would understand to be based on the collection of molecules which create molecular-scale machinery known as proteins, exchanging the currency of energy on an intermolecular scale, life can also be considered from the non-chemical perspective. This section will stretch outside the oft-cited definition for life as “a self reproducing chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution”. This view may allow us to glean insights into “life as we don’t know it”, but it will also help us understand the biosignatures we use here, and perhaps devise new methods for detecting chemical life. We would be remiss not to mention the non-chemical aspect of the biosignature science so crucial to this thesis. In this section, we will break down the current understanding of chemical life from the viewpoint of physicists [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-306'>306</a>]. Then, we will apply these broader frameworks for life to the biosignatures we use in this work.</p>

<p>A few definitions of life have been described by physicists, which we will briefly mention. Erwin Schrödinger mentions in his book <em>What</em> <em>is</em> <em>life?</em> that “Living matter evades the decay to thermodynamical equilibrium by having a metabolism; that is, by exchanging material with its environment, and so escaping the tendency to disorder.” [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-307'>307</a>] Another physicist, Paul Davies, has written that “The way life manages information involves a logical structure that differs fundamentally from mere complex chemistry.” [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-308'>308</a>] Neither of these comments are definitions, but they are still observations of a fundamental component of life, and valuable ones at that. A biosignature detection method may be devised which measures the entropy or ordering of a localized area compared to the average of a larger area, and where a significant discrepancy is found one may probe further to determine if this order is derived randomly or if it is due to life. But how does one perform this next interrogation, of whether it is due to life? This is where our next segment aims to provide value. It is over a course of time, by observing how long it persists, not only in a stable environment but also in response to external stressors, that we assert life must be then evaluated. Ideally, the best observation, we argue, is the observation of adaptation to an environment. In a chemical life example, the response to increasing hypersalinity induces microbes to accumulate osmolyte compounds in high concentrations.</p>

<p>The chemical biosignatures we mention in this thesis are justified well by the physics viewpoint, that disorder is escaped and that logical structure is obtained. We see this chemical order in the use of only a subset of compounds for life to work with. Instead of the rich chemical inventory of meteoritic kerogen, which fills chemical space to a much broader degree and decreases in abundance with increasing carbon number, life incorporates only those compounds which have a suitable role in its dogma [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-309'>309</a>].</p>

<p>For example, the fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) we detected in the samples in Western Australia had patterns of even-to-odd length carbon, a selection of ideal properties for the construction of a cell membrane. The life on Earth enriches itself and the environment with only those biomolecules that are useful to it (or which are products of its reactions to gain energy). Xenobiotic organic carbon which does not fit into the metabolic pathways of life is either sequestered or demolished, due to its toxicity risk [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-310'>310</a>]. Life only wants to work with those compounds which are compatible with its mechanisms, otherwise these foreign compounds may sabotage reactions.</p>

<p>This is a strong biosignature: the observation of an abiotically unlikely case, where these organic compounds that are present on a planetary body are only a small, clearly functional, seemingly evolutionarily selected subset of the organic compounds abundant in meteoritic material across the solar system (Figure 6.2). Current biosignature searches, however, are wearing blinders to this approach.</p>

<p>Preservation is the key aspect which allows us to detect non-extant life. This is because these signs of past life – these kinetically non-favorable chemical distributions – degrade over time [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-164'>164</a>]. They tend to lose the information which they were imbibed with when they were emplaced by life. The existence of these chemical distributions belies the past presence of a complex chemical reaction which selected specific organic compounds for usage in machinery for reproduction (or at least adaptive persistence). Phrased another way, these statistical distributions of chemicals are a biosignature because the are only likely to exist in an environment because of life in the past. Two of the strategies used in this thesis for looking for chemical signs of life, determining the fatty acid and proteinogenic amino acid distributions present, do not necessarily discriminate against the detection of dormant or extinct life which lived long ago and has since ceased all function. They do however look for patterns in these compounds, which may change over time, depending on the preservation of their constituent compounds in the environment. As an inventory of chemical patterns degrades it is taken by entropy from its highly ordered state to a less ordered, more kinetically governed pattern, with some signs of biology still preserved among the molecular rubble. Eventually, this signal will, like a castle of sand, turn only to a pile, a less descriptive arrangement much more easily made from random movement by the wind, and far less functional.</p>

Instruments are tuned for the<em> detection</em> of life, not the non-detection. They are brought to the Atacama desert and</p>

Recently, work considering this has begun to appear [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-191'>191</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-311'>311</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-312'>312</a>], but it by no means dominates the field. Many still speak of optimizing for detection of certain compound classes, however these classes are predisposed to be selections of biological compounds as procuring standards for those compounds is easier, cheaper, and most importantly has a testable benchmark, as opposed to using a barely characterized meteoritic inventory every time.</p>

<p>expect xenobiotics in our samples. And in both cases, we are only looking at aqueously soluble organics. Therefore, can we claim that we have shown life uses only a subset of compounds when we ourselves have in fact selected the list of targets? I would argue no. A more comprehensive work would perform analyses which actually test this hypothesis, does this sample look like an evolutionarily distinct selection of organics? And, would my method prevent me from seeing the difference anyway? The distinction between a targeted and an nontargeted analysis is crucial here, where an nontargeted view would allow us to better characterize chemical space with fewer biases, and thus see the changes imposed by selection in that chemical space (Figure 6.1).</p>

<p>Astrobiologists should look for life’s narrowing of the chemical space, as it uses relative less than what is in meteorite inventories. Therefore, I argue that the detection of enantiomeric excess in amino acids, is an unnecessary analytical difficulty constantly pined after by astrobiologists. Its sufficient instead just to look for structural selection by life by mapping the breadth of the amino acids present. Looking for chiral structural selection is far too analytically challenging. Additionally, the residence time of enantiomeric excess in geologic samples can vary and limited excesses have been observed in meteoritic organic materials [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-313'>313</a>]. If instead, one obtains the capability to distinguish between a wide meteoritic distribution of diverse amino acids and a biologically selected set of amino acids with sufficient accuracy and coverage of chemical space, the biosignature for biological selection of function can be found without invoking a search for chirality.</p>

<p>Crucially, if isotope analysis using high resolution mass spectrometry is included in this strategy, as opposed to chiral separation using unique stationary phases or derivatization which may contaminate and complicate the analysis workflow, an obvious enrichment in lighter isotopes would likely be present in those organic compounds which the pattern consists of, and which are still untouched by entropy, while those which are unused by biology are not enriched to such a degree [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-247'>247</a>]. Isotope measurements are always a comparison between the original source enrichment and the putative biological enrichment, which is difficult to ascertain with single measurements, but a significant gulf between the biologically selected organics and the organics not used biologically on that planetary body may provide a reprieve from this concern [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-314'>314</a>]. Cosine similarity index may be a simple statistical test used as a metric for comparing the biological and abiotic cases, assuming the data from some certainly abiotic sample from that planetary body is on hand to compare with [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-315'>315</a>].</p>

<p>This is potentially a single analysis which may yield two extremely strong biosignatures<sup>1</sup>. Even without confidently identifying the structure from the high resolution mass feature it would still be possible to discern this entropically unlikely arrangement. If laser desorption ionization-mass spectrometry is used here, heavy and complicated separation methods may be done away with, as high-resolution separation by the mass analyzer would be sufficient. Adding an element of ion mobility determination would further allow chemical space to be visualized based on the collisional cross section (CCS) of the compounds, as that molecular mass and CCS are generally correlated, but not perfectly (see appendix).</p>

The index, derived mathematically from any chemical structure, is used to denote how difficult it would be for an individual compound to exist without evolutionary intervention, i.e. continuous selection for advantageous properties. The more moles of a very high molecular assembly index compound you have, the less likely it will have occurred through abiotic chemical systems. Why is that? Perhaps because abiotic chemical systems do not go down the rabbit holes of specificity which are rewarded by evolutionary cycles. When a compound or system which uses it is advantageous, its predecessors are likely also slightly advantaged, and its future iterations are possibly more advantaged. Thus the persistence of a complex molecule and its close structural relatives is driven over time and evolutionary cycles, producing more and more of them. A study of our results with this lens, particularly our mass spectrometric data, would likely yield compounds below this threshold given that the compounds targeted by our study are small. However, a Bayesian combination of the statistical likelihoods of certain molecular patterns to occur abiotically and of the molecular assembly value of each molecule in the pattern may yield more flexible thresholds.</p>

<p>Technosignatures, signs of technology used most often by astronomers in the search for life outside our solar system, bear a similar concept [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-317'>317</a>, <a href='/thesis/references/#ref-318'>318</a>]. They are a sign which may be discovered far after the technological civilization has gone, or may be observed in the present, and they are only likely to have come into existence thanks to a biological system. This could come in the form of a Dyson sphere, detected via the waste heat signature around a star [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-319'>319</a>], a complex message from a distant stellar system, or in the existence of a large monolith in our own solar system [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-320'>320</a>]. Arguably, the distinction between a biosignature and a technosignature is only the extent to which natural selection has occurred on a system. The use of only a subset of all the possible meteorically delivered amino acids for use in proteins by our microbial ancestors was not a choice by conscious apes, but it was arguably analogous to a technological advancement needed for the creation of proteins. Life itself, the behavior exhibited by living matter, is in itself an emergent property of that arrangement of matter in that particular environment.</p>

<h2>6.4 Life is a Behavior</h2>

<p>”The cat was only an exterior, life had posed as a cat.”</p>

<p>–Yukio Mishima,<em> The Sailor Who Fell from Grace With the Sea</em></p>

<p>One technology that life has excelled at developing is communication, and from this, cooperation. This has led to the existence of ecosystems, networks of cooperative interaction between communities of different species. Ecosystems not only respond dynamically to stimuli, nudges from environmental stressors, but change their behavior in the long run to better survive in certain conditions [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-321'>321</a>]. This may require the extinction of a species, the adaptation of another to a new niche, or the creation of a symbiotic or parasitic relation-</p>

<p>ship between species. Individuals of a species in such an environment handle this through gene expression and transcription of certain response proteins, while the species community evolved to contain more individuals which are suited to survival in that environment. Indeed, an ecosystem composed of many levels of community will adapt to changes in its environment as well, becoming increasingly robust to the stressors which frequently challenge it [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-322'>322</a>]. These network effects are crucial to our understanding of how biology works, not just within an ecosystem, but down to the cellular scale where information is used, and up to the scale of a free-market system of international commerce<sup>2</sup>.</p>

<p>Observing the existence of any complex, meaningful network effects which adapt and</p>

<figure><img src='/thesis/06-09.webp' alt='Figure' loading='lazy'></figure>

<p>change based on their environment would be evidence of life (Figure 6.5). While feedback loops exist abiotically on Earth and likely on other planetary bodies and even arguably within stars, I suggest here that a distinct difference between life-like system and non-life is the antifragility of the system. As described in the writing of economist Nassim Nicholas Taleb, antifragile things benefit and grow in response to volatility, shocks, and stressors [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-323'>323</a>]. This distinguishes them from the fragile, which suffer in response to these events, and the robust, which do not significantly gain nor lose from these events. Antifragile systems include biological organisms and the networks they create such as communities of organisms, ecosystems, and social structures. The organisms’ adaptation to the stressor, its benefit from the shocks, comes from behavioral or gene-activated adaptation, while the community and ecosystem responds by changing the components, their relative ratios, and how they interact with each other or the environment. If broadening the definition of life to non-chemical domains we are left with behavior: self-sustaining systems that exhibit Darwinian evolution, regardless of chemistry. We track a number of behavioral traits which are gained by any system through natural selection (persistence against change over time). Instead of examining static chemical signatures, considering dynamic responses of systems gives us more scientifically rich and repeated information about the system at hand. Prebiotic systems do not to persist for long periods of time given their fragility, while abiotic and living systems persist.</p>

<p>The other two aspects of the triad described by Taleb, fragility and robustness, correspond well to prebiotic systems and abiotic systems. The prebiotic system would presumably balance on a razors edge of metabolic capability given its state in an environment perfectly suited for it to take advantage of available energy and its inability to adapt to live in any other conditions. A change in pH for instance may deactivate a chemical reaction which reproduces a set of chemicals for a catalyzed feedback loop, and thus it ceases. While this conjures in the imagination a set of self-catalyzing chemical reactions in a prebiotic soup, examples of fragile behavior which are not at all on the scale of biological chemistry</p>

<p>exist on earth, with one being the so called “Oklo reactor”, a naturally occurring feedback loop of self-regulating nuclear fission [<a href='/thesis/references/#ref-324'>324</a>]. However, this was only self-stabilizing for so long, until it ceased its activity due to depletion of fuel and buildup of neutron absorbing fission products. A robust system would look indifferent to any stressor and its continuous persistence would be independent of the stress.</p>

<p>Emergent effects in groups of individuals, such as those demonstrated by swarming insects, birds, or humans are behaviors of information transfer which likely would not occur abiotically. They are arguably in themselves a technosignature, and would be considered, if observed in some manner independent of their constituent parts, a biosignature, however it may instead be better categorized as a pre-biosignature. The line between prebiotic behavior and biotic behavior is drawn by adaptation and crucially this must be observed within the context in which the system exists.</p>

<p>What is considered worth study by astrobiologists seems to only reach into the systems which include feedback loops, though it arguably all exists on a gradient of persistence (Figure 6.6). In the end, by expanding our definition of life, we unveil non-biological instances of life-like behavior in the universe. This may allow us to overcome the “n=1” issue of biosignature science, and to observe origin of life-like behavior in higher-domain, non-chemical systems. I postulate that analytical-style approaches, modeling an instrument as a source, object, and detector, will still allow us to approach analysis of non-chemical systems.</p>

<p>In the context of biosignature detection, looking for antifragility offers a paradigm shift: instead of seeking static chemical signatures in the form of statistically unlikely patterns (which we agree can represent past or present life), we may return to a model where we consider the dynamic responses themselves as indicative of life in order to avoid false positives due to unforeseeable matrix effects or limited, targeted assays. The<em> Viking</em> experiments are the only instance of a life detection experiment implemented in an extraterrestrial environment, and it could be said that it searched for behavioral biosignatures more so than current proposed approaches do. We posit that behavioral signatures of antifragility will provide a more reliable biosignatures than static chemical or morphological evidence, and one that can be detected with a narrower observation of only a few compounds and their change in response to an environmental stressor. We would anticipate finding not merely robust survival of a biological pattern but innovative responses to stressors reflected in the pattern of compounds.</p>

<p>Therefore, observation of behavioral changes of a system in response to stressors, particularly of adaptation to the stressor allowing for better growth (antifragility) may prove one of the only robust biosignatures. However, observing antifragility in a preserved sample of presumed extinct life would require significant understanding of the taphonomic variables present affecting both our understanding of the putative pre-stressed and poststressed population’s signal, and the degree of stress on each, either over space or time.</p>

<p>As a reach in this direction, we attempted to observe evidence of Darwinian evolution, adaptation, and thus antifragile response by life to in the change in the organic compound inventory across water activity, thus stressor-space. Yet, this was done all within the same small selection of proteinogenic amino acid targeted CE-LIF analyses. In this work, in Chapter 3, we performed an analysis of the change in the whole expressible proteome (in the form of sums of amino acid residues in the proteins) of the microbes across a gradient of water activity. We then compare this to the observed amino acids in the samples obtained via CE-LIF. Amino acids are the building blocks for proteins, and thus their relative abundances will vary based on the construction of the protein. As mentioned in the chapter, some amino acid residues are used in response to specific chemical stresses. Observing an increase in these in a hypersaline system on another planet would indicate adaptation generally, and more specifically antifragility.</p>

<p>The hypothesis we claim to test is whether there is similarity between the two, as this would constitute evidence that evolutionary adaptations in the proteome by microbes under changing environmental stresses are visible in the organic compound inventory (the metabolome) in the lake. A moderate correlation was found<sup>3</sup>. If this connection of amino acid pattern to environmental adaptation proves consistent and strong in future work, then the amino acid pattern change across stressor-space would be a chemical biosignature, and potentially usable as in biosignature detection schema for missions to gradients of extreme environments on other planets. This would perhaps relieve us the need to detect biosignatures with nontargeted means.</p>

<p>When performing analysis across various types of geochemical stressors it is crucial to constrain the effect of those stressors on the analytical method. In the case of our analysis, the assumption that matrix effects were not primarily responsible for the significance observed was not sufficiently proven. This warrants further work on CE-LIF and other methods to ensure that relative amino acid detectability does not vary based on salt composition.</p>

For example, on a planet with episodic high ionizing radiation exposure an antifragile approach would look for systems that not only resist degradation by these shocks but also utilize the energy from ionizing radiation in a way that promotes self sustaining chemical reaction networks<sup>4</sup>.</p>

<p>In conclusion, this thesis aims to increase the detectability of life in hypersaline systems on Earth and on other planets using organic compound detection. I encourage readers to seek out work by my collaborators on the OAST team, which includes incubation analyses that observe microbial uptake of labeled carbon, proving active metabolic incorporation into cellular structures and demonstrating activity more clearly. While that work is a clearer smoking gun of extant biology, and hearkens back to the<em> Viking</em> experiments, where labeled compounds were released to be consumed by Martian metabolisms, it is unable to target signs of past life. Across the universe, a combination of matrix-robust spatially and temporally resolved analyses should be used to determine the presence of antifragile systems like life.</p>
