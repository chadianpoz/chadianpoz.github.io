<p><strong>Abstract</strong></p>
<p>A unique presentation of desert varnish in the American Southwest exhibits properties suggesting non-biological computation, reprinting increasingly complex patterns of etching through surface-to-surface reflection. This first multidisciplinary study of geological, biological, and computational aspects suggests the “Mockroot Canyon Site” exhibits a novel manifestation of naturally arising, self-sustaining emergent behavior. The varnish, composed of clay minerals mixed with iron and manganese oxides, is etched away from the underlying sandstone in fractal patterns (<em>f</em> coefficient approaching 1.67) on two vertically opposed two-dimensional planes. Geometric analysis of curvilinear patterning at numerous scales found solar cycling is an internal clock mechanism for the “refreshing” of this natural circuit board. Solar etching was shown through laboratory experiments to only be effective when lensed through presumed waterfall features nearby. Though microbial involvement in initial varnish formation is likely, present varnish growth is not observed, only solar etching, which appears entirely absent a biological link, animal, microbial or anthropological. This discovery is the first of its kind in non-biological information processing and pattern evolution, challenging traditional definitions of life. </p>
<p><strong>Introduction</strong></p>
<p>In the arid American Southwest desert varnish (DV), a thin brown patina found on the surfaces of rocks in arid regions, is a common occurrence [1,2]. Recent studies have established its formation through the deposition of iron and manganese oxide rich clays by layering of microbial oxidation metabolisms on thousand-year timescales [2,3]. Etching in DV has been observed in the form of petroglyphs left by ancient cultures particularly in the American Southwest [1,4,5,6]. This “natural canvas” is an extremely durable patina which has been known to persist for thousands of years [3,6]. </p>
<p>Though a dark material, due to its metallic sheen the reflectivity of DV is higher than that of surrounding material [2]. Observed by Charles Darwin during his excursions from the HMS Beagle, he wrote that this material “glitters most brilliantly when turned before light” [7]. Here we begin the investigation of extraordinary DV etching using an array of observational, laboratory, and simulation techniques to investigate whether it is a purely geophysical/geochemical feature and rule out anthropological or biological explanations for the patterns present.</p>
<p><strong>Site Geomorphology</strong></p>
<p>In a relatively “mundane” effort to characterize the metagenomic and mineralogical mechanisms underlying DV, our team located three sites of interest for sampling and study of DV formation. The first location visited for study was named the “Mockroot Canyon Site” by the team, and includes a slot canyon, part of the eolian “Navajo sandstone” formation, approximately 200-195 Ma, within the larger Glen Canyon group [8]. This seemingly unremarkable site was chosen for its remoteness and the presence of a small, unexplored slot canyon. Upon arrival at the site in March of 2023, the field team discovered a 21 meter deep, 132-meter-long canyon included an oval room three meters long and one meter wide with an 18.3-centimeter diameter circular aperture (western wall aperture, or WWA) 4 meters from the top of the canyons western face. A drop of three meters from the aperture leads to the floor of the room and a lower ceiling at a distance of 10.81 centimeters.</p>
<p>The floor and ceiling are flat and smooth with no cracks and are covered in DV except where it has been etched away in seemingly intricate and complex patterns. In order to preserve the site from damage robotic tools and endoscopic imaging was utilized to “snake” between the surfaces and compose multiscale LIDAR, visual, and multispectral mapping of the room and its surfaces. No patina or sandstone samples were taken from within the room, instead patina was sampled from nearby MC DV believed to be external to the MCP system, though swabbing was performed for metagenomic analyses. DV mineralogical study via spectral data suggests typical, yet highly enriched DV. High-resolution localized LIDAR showed DV layering thickness varying from nonexistent (fully etched) to a uniform 0.9 microns thick. Colorimetric maps show a uniformly strong contrast between the etched and unetched regions. Patterns of etching are clearly visible in imaging, but a full map of each surface required the stitching of 16 images to create a machine readable 2D image, with distortion from variable image angle removed in post-processing. </p>

<p><strong>Absence of Biological Influence</strong></p>
<p>Isotopic and oxidation state analyses of the DV samples from Mockroot Canyon exterior to the room reveal a geochemical composition indicative of ancient deposition events. The ratios of manganese and iron isotopes, and oxidation states within the metal-rich clays align with those expected from varnish that has undergone little to no recent biogenic activity. These isotopic data suggest that the DV present at this site was deposited &gt;10 Ma, under environmental conditions that may have been more conducive to microbial action than those currently observed.</p>
<p>Despite exhaustive sequencing efforts targeting the 16S ribosomal RNA gene, known for its utility in identifying a broad spectrum of microbes, the results indicate an absence of microbial taxa associated with active DV formation, i.e. members of the <em>Xenococcaceae</em> family of Cynanobacteria, or specifically <em>Chroococcidiopsis</em> [3,9]. This lack of detection persists despite employing both culture-dependent and culture-independent methods, which commonly identify microbial inhabitants in similar arid biome contexts. The microbial assemblages detected do not include those known to contribute to DV formation, such as manganese-oxidizing bacteria, which are essential for the biogenic accumulation of the varnish's characteristic mineral layers. Due to the lack of evidence for presently forming DV we rule out a “DV growth” origin of the MCP and instead opt for a “DV subtraction” explanation. The absence of contemporary biogenic deposition and the presence of older DV layers suggest that the MC system transitioned from a once biologically deposited facade to a unique geophysical structure isolated from the exterior canyon ecosystem.</p>
<p>The presence of a canyon at the site was not documented until aerial surveys were studied in preparation for the DV project. No evidence of plant or fungal life was present in or nearby to the room. This room is barely accessible even with modern rappelling and canyoneering equipment, has only one visible entry and exit point, and thus is likely not to have been influenced by humans, modern or ancient. The MCP do not resemble any known anthropological motifs for desert varnish petroglyphs or rock engravings of any kind. One instance of animal life was present within the MCP room, a deceased hummingbird, identified as <em>Calypte costae</em> [10] through matching to images of the remaining bones. While it is theoretically possible that these patterns are due to bird or insect behavior, we find no evidence to suggest that the individual or any other organisms were involved in the etching processes.</p>
<p><strong>Proposed Etching Dynamics</strong></p>
<p>Endoscopic microscopy of the DV etching patterns at Mockroot Canyon (herein Mockroot Canyon Patterns, or MCP) exhibited evidence of solar radiative weathering (discoloring and changes in reflectivity, sharp-edged wafering). Though simple heating of the varnish was not capable of this level of high-resolution etching we hypothesized that the angle of incident sunlight when travelling through episodic water flow is lensed and focused through the WWA. A geological formation indicative of regular waterflow in the line-of-sunlight to the WWA in the MC exists across the canyon to the South-East wall (southern aperture feature SAF) as an eroded area which crosses the canyon’s formation waterflow direction at a near 90-degree angle, indicating later diagenetic erosion of the wall. However, it is likely that waterflow which formed the SAF is not the waterflow which allowed for close focusing of solar radiation into the WWA, but instead the existence of the SAF and the nearby waterfall over this aperture created a natural lensing effect with two constricting apertures. To test this hypothesis we ensured line of sign feasibility of these apertures and compared them to solar radiation inflection angles, which proved suitable.</p>

<p>The DV's response to solar radiation was measured, showing typical coefficients of thermal heating as compared with DV at other sites. Laboratory heating experiments using a light source and optical lens setup along with DV from a nearby region of MC indicates etching occurs after heating to a surface temperature of 356 Kelvin for a period on the scale of seconds (Figure 2). A computational model was developed to simulate the MCP pattern formation based on environmental input variables. This model employed a thermal modeling framework to replicate the DV’s response to solar cycling with and without lensing parameters.</p>
<p>Motion of the sun and climactic availability of water for lensing appeared to dictate the initiation of etching patterns, however the distorted analemma motif, the most visibly obvious feature of the MCP, indicated that daily waterflow occurred to allow the focusing effect on a regular basis, thus moving the point of inflection on the DV along an analemma or figure eight shaped path over the course of a year. Orthographic projection of an analemma would occur if consistent lensing of solar radiation was applied to etch a region of the varnish on an almost daily basis at similar solar times over the course of a year. </p>
<p>Further work is needed to understand and constrain the optics of water thickness from the waterfall, the necessity for consistency in water flow rates, and thus the hydrological feasibility of such a system on relevant timescales.</p>

<p><strong>Propagation and Computation</strong></p>
<p>Despite the typical slow accrual of DV via microbial action observed globally, the Mockroot Canyon DV is not currently experiencing growth at any observable rate. This stagnation aligns with the etching model, where the varnish is progressively carved rather than built up. As described above, isotopic signatures within the DV layers point towards historical periods of biological activity that have since ceased, leaving behind what we describe as a geochemically inactive surface undergoing a form of natural information processing driven by solar patterns. </p>
<p>Image analyses revealed that the MCP of the floor and ceiling are perfect inversions of each other, where the DV exists on the floor is the negative space in the MCP of the ceiling. Combined with our etching model, this indicates that the DV reflectivity may be high enough to transmit solar energy across the space between the planes, allowing for transmission of information through sequential etching of DV. The fractal nature of this patterning was immediately obvious upon observation and subjected to cursory quantitative mathematical study below. </p>
<p>The fractal dimension of the DV patterns was quantitatively assessed using box-counting methods. High-resolution images of the DV were analyzed to determine the scaling behavior of pattern complexity with size. The fractal dimension was found to be consistent across different scales, with an average value of 1.67, supporting the presence of complex, self-similar patterns indicative of computational processes. </p>
<p>To contrast the DV's global environmental response with the local interactions seen in traditional cellular automata, a comparative analysis was performed. The DV's response to environmental changes was modeled and compared to simulations of local cellular automata. Unlike local models, the DV system's pattern changes were driven by the global solar input, demonstrating a unique form of information processing that extends beyond local interactions.</p>
<p>Time-series analysis of reflected etching was employed to generate the simulated evolution of DV patterns over multiple solar cycles. The results showed that certain fractal patterns re-emerged with a periodicity that matched the solar cycle, indicating a 'refreshing' mechanism akin to a computational process. This reproduction of patterns reinforces the notion of the DV as a naturally occurring register of environmental inputs.</p>
<p>The bones of the deceased <em>Calypte costae</em> were present on the MCP floor and were “traced” by the etching. Visible “repercussions” of the shape of the intrusion are visible concentrically into the surrounding MCP on both the floor and ceiling, as well as forward along the direction of reflection. We hypothesize this is the system’s reaction to an incursion into its processing space.</p>

<p>The DV system at MC exhibits computational characteristics akin to a two-dimensional cellular automaton. The periodic nature of the solar lensing effect provides a signal regulation mechanism analogous to the tick of a universal Turing machine's clock, driving the system's state transitions. However, unlike traditional cellular automata that rely on local interactions between individuals, the desert varnish system integrates global environmental stimuli into its computational process. This suggests a novel class of natural computational systems, where the input tape of a Turing machine is substituted by the DV’s responsive surface, and the read-write operations are replaced by the environmental etching effects of focused solar energy. The system's ability to 'refresh' and evolve these patterns over time, without biotic intervention, places it within a unique subset of non-biological, emergent computational models, potentially analogous to a neural network's pattern recognition capabilities, albeit in a geological context.</p>
<p>In summary, the MC site's unique geomorphology creates a natural oculus effect, where focused sunlight, periodically intensified by water acting as a lens, etches into the varnish. This process is quantifiable and shows a direct correlation with the observed patterns, indicating that solar energy focused by natural lensing is sufficient to alter the DV without biological mediation. The combination of isotopic and metagenomic evidence points to a historical rather than ongoing process of DV formation at MC. The current DV patterns appear to be remnants of past microbial activity, now maintained and etched by abiotic environmental factors such as solar radiation and episodic water flow. This is supported by lab experiments in DV etching and reflectivity of etched rock.</p>
<p>The geochemical etching phenomenon observed at MC provides an elegant, natural example of computation, akin to cellular automata, governed by macroscopic abiotic environmental systems rather than biological or chemical reactions. The findings at MC bridge have the capacity to expand models of complex emergent phenomenon. This study opens avenues for exploring novel information systems in nature, potentially revolutionizing our approach to understanding the intersection of life, computation, and the environment. </p>
