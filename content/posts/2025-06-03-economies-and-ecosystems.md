<p>From the time of life’s origin on Earth, every cell has been subject to larger, often invisible regimes of control. An ecosystem is one such regime: not a collection of deciding, thinking organisms but a supra-entity that operates on the rules of evolution. </p>
<p>If you are good at persisting over time in an environment, you will keep persisting. If you are bad at it, you will probably perish. The same is true for the ecosystems which contain groups of coordinating “yous” which, in the most evolved ecosystems, are in some sense balanced for the ecosystem to persist. </p>
<p>Give any medium enough interacting parts and a few rules of exchange and something stranger than the sum appears, emergent properties. Competition follows; persistence becomes the name of the game.</p>
<p>If you see something in reality right now, it’s probably because its good at persisting. The cup on the table? It was the best cup available on the market, the company which made the shitty cups went under, and they don’t make them anymore. All the other cup manufacturers started making ones that look like the “best” one.<a href="#footnote-1">1</a></p>
<p>The cups, cells, or ecosystems that game the rules and come up with clever solutions survive longer and, over time, are selected for their talent in staying around. Anti-entropy—local eddies of <em>order</em> in a universe bound for disorder—emerges as a side-effect. That moment, the kink the universe where chemistry begins to conserve information on how to persist; thats the real “spark” of life.</p>
<figure><img src="/figures/life-eddy.svg" alt="Diagram: entropy flows from order to disorder; the life line loops into an eddy, the no-life line runs straight"><figcaption>Life is an eddy current in the entropic flow of the universe towards non-informational, disordered matter.</figcaption></figure>
<p>It is fashionable to imagine first contact as a sleek silver UFO hovering over wheat-fields. But the real alien power we already serve is the global economy— a self-reflexive entity we neither designed<a href="#footnote-2">2</a> nor understand, which now choreographs the desires of eight billion primates. Microbes obey their ecosystems with the same resignation.</p>
<p>Both systems evolved, <strong>not through central planning </strong>(sorry Soviets), but through a blind march through time: trillions of interactions, with reality sifting for forms that refuse to die. </p>
<p>The economy survives because our transactions feed it; the coral reef survives because its symbioses recycle every atom. In each case individuality is tolerated only so long as it oils a larger, emergent logic<a href="#footnote-3">3</a>.</p>

<p>Traditional astrobiology hunts for the chemical footprint of chemical-based life, even sometimes non-carbon or water-based life. Sensible, but narrow. But perhaps biology is not limited to wet chemistry bound by covalent bonds; it is in any case the behaviour of systems that conserve information against the odds. We could concede that “biology” refers to life that arises from chemistry, but also note that a new study of these non-biological lifeforms is warranted.</p>
<p>Human corporations, slime-mould logistics, ant colonies sculpting air-conditioned nests—each demonstrates life-like computation in non-human substrates. They learn, optimise, and rewrite their own operating code to resist collapse. These are building block-agnostic life-forms hiding in plain sight, with similar emergent properties and <strong>all with the same goal: persist</strong>.</p>
<p>Earth’s surface chemistry is draped in biological graffiti; finding pristine “abiotic” chemistry here is difficult. We aim telescopes and probes at Mars, Europa, Enceladus, Titan—locales where Earth-life has (perhaps) not yet overwritten the abiotic chemical record. But even that strategy misses half the stage.</p>
<p>Surely not every orderly pattern deserves the title “life”. For instance, mineral crystals replicate form but cannot pivot when the environment changes. True life is <em><strong>antifragile</strong></em>: it rewrites itself under stress and becomes better adapted to grow<a href="#footnote-4">4</a>. The minimal test, then, is <strong>adaptive persistence</strong>—“does the system learn?”</p>
<p>We may yearn for charismatic extraterrestrials, but the genuine alien has always been emergent order—ecosystems, cultures, economies, ideologies—that have controlled us since the dawn of time. Recognising that fact reframes the astrobiological quest: we are perhaps not looking for life forms, but for dynamics which describe life. They are time resolved and can span a broad range of materials, but still as we know it they are only based on <em>biological</em> life, i.e., chemical life as we know it.</p>
<p>So perhaps our current search, for chemical-based life forms, is worthwhile, as it lets us see the constituent members of more complex higher order systems. However there may be some great value in recognizing the emergence of higher order lifeforms in understanding our own place in the universe, our own free will, and our relationship with the information systems and other lifeforms around us.</p>
<p>It’s just persistence who’s at the wheel, a property of time and space, driving adaptation and, once information storage arrives<a href="#footnote-5">5</a>, evolution.</p>
