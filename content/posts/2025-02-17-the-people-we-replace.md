<p>Sometimes people slip into the same memory slot. <br>Joey looks like Adam, and now I’ll mix them up forever. <br>One face, one mannerism, one carved-out space in my mind—already shaped, waiting. <br>I’ll see Joey laugh and remember Adam’s voice, though I can't recall it. <br>I’ll hear a story and forget who told it, but I’ll know the type of person who did. </p>
<p>Then, months from now, I’ll meet Sam.<br>And without realizing it, Joey will fade into the same outline.<br>I won’t think about Adam anymore, but I’ll still be reacting to him, through Sam.<br>A slight tilt of the head, a way of pausing before speaking—recognition will flicker.<br>Not recognition of Sam, but of the role he’s already been cast in.<br>The continuity of people, the seamless way they replace each other,<br>as if the world refuses to leave an empty space where someone used to be.</p>
<p>We meet people, we hold them for a time, then they leave. <br>And the mind, always efficient, doesn’t leave gaps. <br>Someone else slides in, seamless. <br>We don’t notice the swap, not consciously. <br>A coworker reminds us of an old friend, and we are already comfortable. <br>A stranger’s half-smile triggers something familiar, and we trust too quickly. <br>Or we distrust, because they resemble someone who hurt us once. <br><br>It’s not just romance—though, of course, <br>some people find comfort in those echoes, <br>dating their ex’s twin, their mother’s kindness, their father’s distance. <br>But there’s something more fascinating at the edge of social memory, <br>where one person melts into the next, <br>as we move from city to city, job to job, life to life. <br><br>We never learn their last name. <br>We forget their voice. <br>We don’t think about them again. <br>But their shape stays, waiting to be filled. <br><br>And without trying, we replace them.</p>
