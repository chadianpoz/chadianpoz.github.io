Developing a method for an analytical instrument is slow, grueling work, especially when you’re developing it from scratch. When we were building Otea, finding the right run parameters took more of our time than anything else.

Otea separates compounds using an electric field across a microfluidic channel. Each compound moves at its own speed, set by its charge and size, so they reach the detector at different times. How well it separates depends on a set of voltages and timings, and on how those settings interact with the chip, the buffer and the sample. There are infinitely many possible combinations of these parameters, and only a few will result in a successful run. Getting them right is the difference between clean, resolved peaks and unreadable noise.

To find the ideal parameters faster, we built Juniper: an agentic instrument control loop that reads each Otea run, in the context of the literature and everything we have tried before, and proposes the voltages and timings for the next run. This idea anchors the thesis of Deleon, bringing agentic loops to analytical chemistry.

The best thing is, Juniper is useful far beyond our Otea.

## Starting from trial and error

When we began building Otea, the only voltages we had came from the literature. They were a reasonable starting point, but they were measured on other chips and other setups. Much of our first few months was trial and error.

Once our results became repeatable and stable, we moved to a design of experiments (DoE) model. We ran factorial design across bounded parameter ranges and fit a polynomial model to estimate main effects and two-factor interactions.

That works when the response changes smoothly... but ours did not. Joule heating scales rapidly (with buffer conductivity times the square of voltage), and past a threshold it distorts the separation. That failure boundary is a curve across two factors, which a low-order polynomial fits poorly. DoE can exclude an infeasible region, but only if you know where it is in advance. We didn’t, and because the runs were fixed up front, we kept running settings our earlier results suggested would fail.

## Closing the loop

The first version of Juniper was a Claude conversation. After each run, we sent the parameters, the results and the relevant papers, and asked what to change next. The advice was useful, and it improved as the thread filled with context.

That helped us believe in something bigger. Everything the model needed already existed: the literature, every past discussion and every past data file. It would know far more than any of us could remember about our own data, as long as we gave it the context. A system built on all of it could propose the next parameters the moment a run finished. Then, if a voltage range kept failing, it would move away from it on its own.

Each run would inform the next, and the method would keep evolving until it reached optimal performance.

## How Juniper works

Juniper tunes the capillary electrophoresis runs that separate amino acids on Otea's microfluidic chips. Its work is organized into campaigns, each with a goal (e.g., improve baseline quality, signal-to-noise, or separation between compounds).

**Campaigns and phases.** Each campaign is split into phases. Within a phase, the costly setup stays fixed: chip, dye, buffer, sample and injection time.

**Run settings.** Each experiment changes only voltages and timings. Four control loading: load field, pinch ratio, pinch asymmetry and load time. Three control separation: separation field, pull-back ratio and pull-back asymmetry. Juniper converts these into the voltage delivered to each arm of the chip.

**One experiment at a time.** Juniper proposes a run. Humans run it on Otea and upload the electropherogram. The run is then scored, which informs the next proposal.

**Trade-off space.** In our case, each run is scored on signal-to-noise, reference peak width, migration time and a few other metrics. A qualitative score adds yes/no checks: was the run valid, did the baseline stay flat, did the expected peaks show up.

**Evidence checking.** Juniper plots traces, compares runs and searches the papers we have uploaded, so every recommendation points to evidence for human verification.

## Research is moving from hypothesis to discovery

For most of its history, biology has moved one hypothesis at a time. Experiments are expensive, so scientists bet carefully, and every bet was shaped by human intuition, with our blind spots and biases. Whole regions of what’s possible go unexplored because no one thought to look there.

Juniper points to a different paradigm. When a system can read every past run, every relevant paper and every failed attempt, and propose the next experiment the moment the last one ends, scientists no longer have to choose between being careful and being ambitious.

This is not about one instrument. Every lab that develops a method, optimizes a protocol or searches a large parameter space loses months the way we did. A loop like this gives those months back.

We built Juniper to work on Otea, but what we found is a way of doing science that moves faster than any of us could alone, and we believe every scientist should have it.
