<p><strong>Scale One: Simple Crystals</strong></p>
<p>Consider ritonavir and the infamous, catastrophic “crystal‐seeing” phenomenon, brought to my attention in <a href="https://lavo.ai/blog/avoiding-ice-ix">this post</a> by Dr. Derek Metcalf. In the 1990s, only one crystal form (ritonavir I) of the HIV drug (<a href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/12/Ritonavir_structure.svg">an organic molecule</a>) ritonavir was known and used in its formulation. Then, a new, more stable polymorph (ritonavir II) suddenly appeared. The existence of this more stable form basically caused all ritonavir I to collapse into ritonavir II.</p>
<blockquote><p>After many years of research, production, and distribution, in 1998, drug production lines begun to show problems related to “undesirable” crystal formation in a series of production batches that failed the dissolution test. An investigation of the reason for the failure showed the unexpected appearance of a new crystalline form of ritonavir that affected the way the drug dissolved, hence its absorption.</p></blockquote>
<p>Even trace amounts of the mysterious ritonavir II acted as seed crystals, irreversibly converting batches of the drug to a form with much lower solubility and bioavailability. It was a chemical massacre. This dramatic change—a minor structural alteration at the molecular level—led to a full‐scale transformation of the drug’s performance (it had to be re-engineered as a soft capsule drug!). Ritonavir I can no longer be successfully manufactured en masse, anywhere (in solution phase, that is, through the solid phase it can be as its more stable there). <strong>It illustrates how minute initial “seeding” events in crystallization can lead to irreversible, large‐scale changes, on a planetary scale.</strong></p>
<figure><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4R3S!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F17081d37-7b29-45e6-bf6b-64ee44a587fa_1173x674.png" alt=""><figcaption>Crystal forms of Ritonavir. Credit: <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms23169013">Braga et al., 2022</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>A similar principle operates in biological polymers with prions.</strong> Prions are misfolded proteins that act as templates, compelling normally folded proteins to adopt their aberrant structure. Much like the seed crystals of ritonavir, prions spread by inducing a conformational change in nearby molecules, leading to a cascade of misfolding that underlies serious neurodegenerative diseases. In both cases, a small “infectious” entity—whether a crystal seed or a misfolded protein—sets off a self‐reinforcing chain reaction. An unstoppable cascade, given the size and the immense power of a single seed.</p>

<p><strong>Scale Two: Instructional Systems</strong></p>
<p>Viruses, too, exemplify the principle of seeding, <strong>though through a more advanced information transmission path.</strong> Unlike prions and crystal seeds, viruses don't merely propagate by altering physical structures, forcing individual molecules into lower energy states. Instead, viruses hijack the very genetic code of a cell. Upon infection, a virus injects its RNA or DNA into a host cell, redirecting the cell’s machinery to replicate the virus’s genetic material instead of its own, much like a saboteur’s memo on a factory floor<a href="#footnote-1">1</a>.</p>
<figure><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SsVt!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdbd6d06c-1868-475a-8e2d-4f91062d9936_2000x1564.jpeg" alt="Bacteriophages"><figcaption>Bacteriophages injecting genetic information into a cell. Credit: Eye of Science.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The virus replaces the cell’s natural biological processes with the task of reproducing the virus’s code and its mechanism for attack (it’s familiar encapsulating structure and claw-like cell membrane landing gear). It takes the energy (ATP) and matter (complex, fixed carbon) which the cell has set aside for its own growth and redirects it towards the task of virus creation, through what is essentially a cyberattack<a href="#footnote-2">2</a>.</p>
<p>The viral genome (virome) speaks the same language as the cell’s own genetic code, ensuring the viral message is translated into destructive action. While a tiny seed crystal can turn an entire batch of ritonavir into an unusable form, a virus transforms the cell into a <em>factory</em> of replication, spreading exponentially with each new infected cell, all while altering the host at a molecular and systemic level. Once initiated, the system continues to propagate the infection globally, an unstoppable cascade powered by information. </p>
<p>However this time, it’s not only at the crystal scale, it’s not just a forced mirroring or a cajoling pull to a lower energy state, it takes place both in the machinery and in the information. <strong>Its at a higher level of order, or of complexity, or of scale.</strong> For a crystal or folded protein, this information and the matter itself are synonymous, actualized/encoded in its shape. For the cell, the information is stored in polymers of nucleic acid, which dictate the machinery which are built and perform the tasks of homeostasis, growth, and reproduction.<a href="#footnote-3">3</a></p>

Credit: Biology LibreTexts.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Now, a higher level, the genetic code, combined with the essential machinery of the surrounding cell, is too seeding. Conventional life is reproducing. By converting the material around it into fuel, metabolizing, growing, and reproducing its genetic code, it  is behaving like these seeds, but unlike crystal seeds, the virus and the cell have a special property. <strong>They have the capability to adapt in response to stressors and to adapt. They are antifragile, and they evolve.</strong> More on this concept in another post.</p>
<p><strong>Stage Three: Social Networks</strong></p>
<p>Seeding events are chain reactions that radically transform everything in a system. They work up and down the scale to have huge impacts.</p>
<p>Now I’d like to posit another, less direct point: that <strong>in the cultural sphere, anything from a seemingly offhand remark or a deliberately incendiary meme can act as a “<a href="https://slatestarcodex.com/2018/10/30/sort-by-controversial/">scissor statement</a>,” slicing through rational debate and polarizing public opinion.</strong> </p>
<figure><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7Tc8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2badf954-1064-4c65-9313-0a0eab685296_256x389.jpeg" alt="The dress"><figcaption><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_dress">The Dress,</a> what colors do you see?</figcaption></figure>
<p>At the cultural or social level, <strong>memes are the informational analogues of physical objects </strong>(crystals are objects). Memes are ideas or cultural units that propagate from mind to mind, often evolving along the way. Most memes are relatively slow and mild in their spread, but others—like these “scissor statements”—are particularly potent<a href="#footnote-4">4</a>.  A scissor statement is an utterance that sharply polarizes human opinion, slicing through nuanced debate and seeding entrenched divisions. <strong>Such ideas become infohazards, the prions and the seeding crystals of the information world.</strong> They carry the risk of triggering widespread social system harm or good, much as prions and crystal seeds can irreversibly alter a brain system or a drug capsule.</p>
<p>The common underlying principle: whether in chemistry, biology, or sociology, is that  seeding can lead to runaway transformations. Whats the utility here? Recognizing this analogy may inspire novel approaches to controlling unwanted cascades. Applied seeding-ology.<a href="#footnote-5">5</a></p>
<p>Politicians have learned to exploit this dynamic most successfully. They know that the reward mechanism in modern electoral politics isn’t about nuanced policy or measured discussion; they’re just aiming to trigger an emotional wildfire. For the 2010s-2020s US, controversial, enraging social issues are the currency of power. By stoking anger and fear, politicians and the corporations that support them galvanize a base that is <strong>primed to donate money, vote in droves, and spread their message across the digital landscape</strong>. The algorithms that govern our social media feeds, favoring sensational and emotionally charged content, only magnify these efforts. In this way, every inflammatory tweet, every conspiracy-laden Facebook post, acts as a seed that, if repeated and amplified, reshapes our collective reality.</p>
<figure><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!MOZ-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe7009c5b-fe63-42fd-967c-be9e0c3f5abe_1200x1091.jpeg" alt="Graph showing different model scenarios"><figcaption><a href="https://arstechnica.com/science/2020/02/divided-we-fall-how-ant-behavior-mimics-political-polarization/">Models</a> of social structures show polarization dynamics. <a href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsif.2019.0564">Tokita and Tarnita 2020</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>On the other end of the spectrum, a kind of self-perpetuating culture of obsessive self-victimization and hyper-awareness of systemic injustice can take root. The narrative of perpetual victimhood is a lens through which every social interaction is interpreted. This narrative, like a contagious meme, reinforces an identity. It creates an echo chamber where every news story validates an interpretation of society as fundamentally broken—<strong>fueling a cycle where every setback is seen as evidence of a sentient, evil, oppressive power structure, rather than a complex interplay of otherwise neutral forces.</strong></p>
<p>In both cases, these processes are underpinned by reward mechanisms which have <strong>increasingly incentivized politicians on both sides to embrace incendiary issues that inflame and enrage citizens</strong>. Harnessing outrage has become a calculated strategy: controversial statements drive donations, boost voter turnout among a mobilized base, and secure media attention. The focus on systemic victimization can serve to validate a communal identity built on the perception of constant oppression. Both phenomena rely on an <strong>information ecosystem where seeding sets off cascades of behavior that reshape political and social landscapes.</strong></p>
<p>Dr. Yuval Noah Harari has argued that <strong>society is defined by its narratives</strong>, while filmmaker Adam Curtis presents in his documentary “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt86SSNQKPw">Hypernormalisation</a>” that <strong>increasingly they have become simplistic, polarized, and often dangerously detached from objective reality</strong>. The danger is compounded by the fact that our political reward systems are designed to favor outrage. Politicians are incentivized to use issues that trigger immediate emotional responses rather than fostering thoughtful discourse.</p>
<p>To counter these dynamics, we must address the deep cultural incentives that drive us toward divisive narratives. <strong>Recognizing that these seeding dynamics—whether in a laboratory, a cell, or a social network—are the root of both biological and societal transformation is crucial.</strong> Only by reexamining our reward mechanisms and the cultural structures that support them can we hope to cultivate a public sphere where the integrity of our shared intersubjective realities are preserved, if that is indeed what we should strive for...</p>
